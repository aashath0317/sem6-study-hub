## What data mining is about

:::key Short definition
Data mining is the process of discovering **interesting** patterns or knowledge from **massive amounts of data**. "Interesting" means **non-trivial, implicit, previously unknown and potentially useful**.
:::

The lesson introduction describes data mining as:

- An **iterative process** where progress means **discovery**, made **automatically or manually**.
- Most useful in **exploratory analysis**, where you have **no fixed idea** of what an "interesting" result will be.
- The **search for new, valuable and non-trivial information** in large volumes of data.
- A **cooperative effort of humans and computers**. The best results come from balancing **human experts' knowledge** (describing problems and goals) with **computers' search power**.

## Why data mining is required

- Huge volumes of data fill our computers, networks and lives. Governments, scientific institutions and businesses spend enormous resources **collecting and storing** it.
- **Only a small amount of it is ever used.** The volumes are too large to manage, or the structures are too complicated to analyse.

:::warn Why does this happen?
The original effort to create a dataset usually focuses on **storage efficiency**. It **doesn't include a plan for how the data will later be used and analysed**.
:::

- Understanding large, complex, information-rich datasets matters in virtually **all fields of business, science and engineering**.
- In business, **corporate and customer data are a strategic asset**. Being able to **extract hidden knowledge and act on it** matters in a competitive world.
- The whole process of applying computer-based methods to discover knowledge from data is called **data mining**.

## Big Data and the 5 V's

- **2005:** **Roger Mougalas** of **O'Reilly Media** first coined the term **"Big Data"**, a year after O'Reilly created the term **Web 2.0**.
- **Big data** means data sets so large they are **almost impossible to manage and process with traditional business intelligence tools**.
- Social networks and Web 2.0 drove the growth. Start-ups and then governments began big-data projects.

:::key Definition
**Big Data** is the large volume of data, **both structured and unstructured**, that floods a business every day. *It's not the amount of data that matters but **what organisations do with it**.*
:::

### The original 3 V's

| V | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | Data collected from **many sources** in huge amounts | Business transactions, social media, **sensor / machine-to-machine** data |
| **Velocity** | Data streams in at **unprecedented speed** and must be handled **promptly** | **RFID tags, sensors, smart metering** in **near-real time** |
| **Variety** | Data comes in **all types of formats** | Structured numeric DB data → unstructured **text, email, video, audio, stock ticker, financial transactions** |

### Two more V's added later

| V | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Veracity** | **Trustworthiness** of the data. More data doesn't mean clean or accurate data. Data must stay **consolidated, cleansed, consistent and current** to support the right decisions. |
| **Value** | **Worth** of the data extracted. Endless data isn't always useful. You must understand the **costs and benefits** of collecting and analysing it, so the mined data can ultimately be **monetised**. |

:::tip Memory hook
**V**olume = how **much**. **V**elocity = how **fast**. **V**ariety = how many **kinds**. **V**eracity = how **true**. **V**alue = how **useful**.
:::

:::exam
"Explain big data using the V's" is a typical question. Give all **five**, a one-line meaning and an example each. Mention that the first three are the original definition and Veracity and Value were added later.
:::

## Defining data mining properly

### The customer loyalty example
The problem is **fickle customer loyalty** in a competitive market. A database of **customer choices and profiles** holds the answer:

1. Analyse **patterns of former customers** to find the characteristics of those who **left** and those who **stayed loyal**.
2. Use those characteristics to **identify current customers likely to "jump ship"**.
3. Target that group with **special treatment**. This would be **too costly** to give the whole customer base.
4. On the positive side, the same techniques find customers likely to want **another service** the company offers, so they can be targeted with special offers.

> In today's competitive, customer-centred economy, **data is the raw material that fuels business growth**, if only it can be mined.

### The formal definition

:::formula
**Data mining** = discovering **interesting** (non-trivial, implicit, previously unknown, potentially useful) **patterns or knowledge** from **massive amounts of data**
:::

The four requirements:

- The process must be **automatic or (more usually) semi-automatic**.
- The patterns must be **meaningful**: they lead to some **advantage**, usually **economic**.
- The data is always present in **substantial quantities**.
- Useful patterns let us make **non-trivial predictions on new data**.

### How are patterns expressed?

:::grid
**Black box**

The inside is **effectively incomprehensible**. It makes predictions, but you can't see why.

*e.g. a complex neural network*
+++
**Transparent box**

Its construction **reveals the structure of the pattern**. You can read and understand it.

*e.g. a decision tree or rule "IF age < 25 AND student THEN buys laptop"*
:::

Both make useful predictions. The difference is whether a human can **understand** the pattern.

## Applications of data mining

Data mining is used in banking, stock exchanges, marketing, retail, signal processing, insurance, telecoms, e-commerce, health, medicine, biology, genetics, industry, construction, education, intelligence, science and engineering. The notes focus on **three sectors**:

### 1. Banking and finance
Used to find **what, when and why** customers prefer.

| Application | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Market basket analysis** | If a customer buys a certain group of items, they're **likely to buy another group**. This helps retailers understand buying patterns and **change the store layout** (e.g. bread near butter). |
| **CRM (Customer Relationship Management)** | Strategies to **acquire and retain** customers. Mining customer data shows **where to focus** on retention. |
| **Fraud detection** | Billions are lost to fraud each year. Use **historical data to model fraudulent behaviour** and flag similar new cases. |

### 2. Health sector
Reduces the complexity of studying healthcare transactions.

| Application | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Measuring treatment effectiveness** | Compare **symptoms, causes and treatments** to find the most effective course of action. Compare patient groups on different **drug regimens** to find what works best and **saves most money**, which helps **standardise treatment**. |
| **Bioinformatics** | Biology is **data-rich**. Uses: **gene finding, protein function inference, disease diagnosis/prognosis, treatment optimisation, protein/gene interaction networks, data cleansing, protein subcellular location prediction**. |
| **Fraud detection** | Establish **normal patterns**, then find **unusual** medical claims by clinics, physicians or labs: inappropriate referrals or prescriptions, insurance fraud. |

### 3. Public sector
Predicting **public safety and security** problems and **optimising decisions**.

| Application | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Weather forecasting** | Build **prediction models from historical data**. Needed for climate monitoring, agriculture, energy planning, aviation and communication. |
| **Criminal investigation** | **Criminology** identifies crime characteristics. Crime data is **high-volume** with **complex relationships**, which suits mining. **Text crime reports** can be converted and used for **crime matching**. |

:::tip Answering "give applications" questions
Name the **sector → application → what is mined → benefit**. For example: *Banking → fraud detection → historical transaction data modelled for fraud patterns → flags new suspicious transactions early.*
:::
