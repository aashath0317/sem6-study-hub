1What is "place"?

Place is the fourth and final element of the marketing mix. It is also called the channel, distribution or intermediary decision. It's the mechanism through which goods and services are transferred from producer to consumer 00:00.

Marketing channel

A marketing channel is a set of interdependent organisations involved in making a product or service available for use or consumption by the consumer.

  • An excellent product at a great price is worthless if it isn't available where and when the customer wants it.
  • Channel decisions mean selecting, managing and motivating intermediaries: wholesalers, distributors, brokers and retailers. They're also called resellers.
  • A firm's channel decisions directly affect every other marketing decision.
  • Sri Lankan examples 01:04: wholesalers buy in bulk from the manufacturer, while supermarkets, pharmacies and grocery shops are retailers that meet the final consumer.
  • Selling with no intermediary is direct marketing 01:28. It can cost more, so firms choose the number of intermediaries based on their capacity and scope.

Session objectives:

  1. Describe the nature of marketing channels and their importance
  2. Identify how channels interact and are organised
  3. Understand the problems in designing and managing channels
  4. Explain the role of physical distribution and supply chain management in attracting and satisfying customers

2The nature of marketing channels

  • Few producers sell directly to end customers. Most use intermediaries to reach the market.
  • Intermediaries add value: they make the product available at lower cost and reach customers country-wide 02:58.
  • They create utilities 03:31: time utility, and storage utility (the manufacturer can't store everything). They sometimes convert or repackage the product.
  • Each layer of intermediaries that moves the product and its ownership closer to the final buyer is a channel level.

3Push and pull strategies

Push

Marketers take the product to the customer by pushing it through the channel. The manufacturer gives sales incentives, discounts and promotions to intermediaries so they push it onto consumers 04:17.

Example: fragrance brands give department stores incentives to push a new fragrance. Customers first meet it in the store.

Best for new brands or new lines that aren't well established.

Pull

Marketers make consumers come to them. Uses word of mouth, mass media promotion and advertised sales promotions to build demand, so consumers ask intermediaries for the product 05:36.

Pull aims at brand loyalty and repeat buying; push is more about the short-term sale.

Intermediaries feed customer insights back to the manufacturer to make pull campaigns more effective.

4How channel members add value

All these functions use scarce resources, are done better through specialisation, and can be shifted between channel members.

Function What the channel member does
Negotiate prices Handles all negotiation with end consumers. Once bought, the product belongs to the channel member
Inventory management Matches stock to demand, and doesn't order stock that won't sell (it blocks inventory and finance)
Communicate with buyers Keeps contact with current and potential customers; may run a CRM
Persuasive communications Local marketing and promotion to build awareness of the buying point
Gather and distribute information About consumers, competitors and the environment, which feeds the firm's strategy
Risk taking Bears the risk of unsold stock, bad debts and other losses
Financing Pays in advance or on credit, often before consumers pay
Physical distribution Gets goods to end users quickly and in good condition

Why use intermediaries?

  • Using them raises product awareness and reaches a larger customer base, so sales grow.
  • They're usually cheaper than doing distribution yourself, giving higher profit and less spent on advertising and promotion.
Reasons for using intermediaries
  • Lack of financial resources
  • Lack of necessary skills
  • Direct marketing isn't feasible
  • To improve distribution efficiency

5Channel levels

Channel level

The number of intermediary levels between producer and consumer. It can be 0, 1, 2, 3 or more.

Level Path Example
Zero-level (direct) Manufacturer → Consumer Telemarketing, company website, own stores
One-level Manufacturer → Retailer → Consumer Car maker → dealer
Two-level Manufacturer → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer FMCG → wholesaler → grocery shop
Three-level Manufacturer → Agent/Distributor → Wholesaler → Retailer → Consumer Imported goods

6Channel behaviour and conflict

  • A channel is a complex behavioural system: firms depend on each other to reach individual, firm and channel goals.
  • Example: a Volvo dealer depends on Volvo to design cars customers want, and Volvo depends on the dealer to sell them. Each dealer's success depends on how well the whole Volvo channel competes with other carmakers' channels.
  • The channel works best when each member does the tasks it does best.
  • But members often focus on their own short-run goals, and disagree on who does what, for what reward. That creates channel conflict.

Horizontal conflict

Between firms at the same level of the channel.

Example: a dealer complains that other dealers in town steal sales by aggressive pricing or by selling outside their territories.

Vertical conflict

Between different levels of the same channel. It's more common.

Example: a manufacturer and its distributors or retailers disagree, as in the pharmaceutical industry, where powerful intermediaries strengthen their negotiating position.

Conflict isn't always bad

Some conflict is healthy competition and can trigger innovation. But for the channel to perform well, roles must be clearly specified and conflict managed, ideally by a firm with the power to assign roles. The goal is a win-win for all members.

7Conventional vs vertical marketing systems

Conventional marketing channel

Manufacturer, wholesaler and retailer are independent businesses, each seeking its own profit. No member controls the others beyond specific contracts.

Example: a supermarket buys from several wholesalers, who each buy from different growers.

Vertical marketing system (VMS)

Producers, wholesalers and retailers act as a unified system. One member owns the others, has contracts with them, or has so much power that they all cooperate.

Examples: Ferrero (Nutella) owns hazelnut suppliers; Delta Airlines owns a jet-fuel refinery.

VMS type How it's coordinated Example
Corporate VMS Ownership of all levels by one firm, through forward integration (towards customers) or backward integration (towards suppliers) Apple selling through its own stores
Contractual VMS Formal contracts between independent firms at different levels; gains economies of scale. The most popular kind Franchising (McDonald's, Burger King), retailer- or wholesaler-sponsored cooperatives
Administered VMS Size and power of one dominant member, informally Walmart setting standards for makers of small products
Horizontal marketing system

Two or more companies at the same level join forces to exploit a new opportunity, combining financial, production or marketing resources.

Examples: a bank's ATMs inside a supermarket; two manufacturers joining to get economies of scale.

Choosing the right system: look at product, environment, customer and company factors, and ask:

  1. Which system gives the best coverage of the target market?
  2. Which gives customers what they want: convenience, variety or service?
  3. Which is the most profitable?

8Designing a marketing channel

  1. Analyse consumer needs
  1. Set channel objectives and constraints
  1. Identify the major channel alternatives
  1. Evaluate the alternatives

1. Analyse consumer needs: five service outputs

Service output Meaning
Lot size How many units a buyer can purchase at one time
Waiting and delivery time How long customers wait for the goods (buyers prefer fast)
Spatial convenience How easy the channel makes it to reach and buy the product
Product variety The range offered (consumers usually prefer more)
Service backup Add-ons such as delivery, credit, installation and maintenance
Trade-off

Higher service outputs mean higher channel costs and higher prices. Discount resellers (online and offline) show that many customers accept lower service to save money.

2. Set objectives and constraints

State objectives in terms of targeted service output levels, while keeping total channel cost down. Different segments may need different service levels.

Objectives are influenced by:

  1. The nature of the company
  2. The products
  3. The marketing intermediaries
  4. Competitors
  5. The environment (economic conditions, legal regulations)

Sometimes a firm sells in the same outlets as competitors; sometimes it avoids their channels.

3. Identify the major alternatives

Each alternative is described by (a) types of intermediaries, (b) number of intermediaries and (c) responsibilities of each member.

(a) Types of intermediaries

Type Role Takes title?
Agents and brokers Facilitate sales and earn a fee or commission. Popular with small firms needing wider distribution No
Merchant wholesalers Buy in bulk, hold inventory, resell to businesses and small/medium retailers Yes
Distributors (functional wholesalers) Advance sales between manufacturer and retailers without buying the products No
Retailers (traditional and online) Sell to the final consumer: corner stores, malls, e-commerce sites Yes

(b) Number of intermediaries

Strategy Meaning Typical products
Intensive Stock the product in as many outlets as possible (place utility) Convenience goods: soap, biscuits, soft drinks
Selective More than a few but fewer than all willing intermediaries Shopping goods: appliances, clothing brands
Exclusive Strictly limit the number of intermediaries, for more aggressive, informed selling and control over price, credit and promotion Luxury and specialty goods: luxury fashion, high-end cars

(c) Responsibilities of each member

Members must be treated fairly and given a chance to be profitable. Responsibilities include risk taking, financing, physical distribution, negotiations, inventory management, contacts, promotions and information.

4. Evaluate the alternatives

The firm's options might be a company sales force, a manufacturer's agency or industrial distributors. Judge each one against three criteria:

Criterion Question
Economic Which gives more sales at lower cost: own sales force or an agency?
Control Using intermediaries means giving up control. How much is acceptable?
Adaptive Can we keep flexibility over the long term vs the short term?

9Managing the marketing channel

Managing a channel means analysing, planning, organising and controlling it. Motivation tools include higher allowances, strong advertising support, higher trade discounts, training, and logistic support.

Task Key points
Selecting members "For consumers, the channels are the company." Check years in business, growth and profit, financial strength, service reputation. For agents, check other lines carried and sales-force quality; for exclusive outlets, check location and type of customer
Training and motivating Treat intermediaries like customers: understand their needs, offer training and capability building
Evaluating Regularly check sales quota attainment, delivery time, average inventory, handling of damaged/lost goods, cooperation in training. Give functional discounts for each agreed service; retrain or counsel weak performers
Modifying arrangements Review when distribution isn't working, buying patterns change, new competitors or channels appear, or the product moves through its life cycle. Add/drop members or channels; use data mining on shopping data

10Marketing logistics and supply chain management

Marketing logistics (physical distribution)

Planning, implementing and controlling the physical flow of goods, services and related information from points of origin to points of consumption to meet customer requirements at a profit.

Logistics type Direction
Outbound Factory → resellers → customers
Inbound Suppliers → factory
Reverse Returned goods: reusing, refurbishing or disposing of broken or excess products

Supply chain management handles the upstream and downstream flows of materials, finished goods and information among suppliers, the company, resellers and final customers.

The goal

Give a targeted level of customer service at the lowest cost. Logistics is about profit, not just sales.

Advantages of good logistics:

  • Reduced delivery time: impresses customers and builds loyalty
  • Pinpointing customer issues: tracking orders and shipments to fix problems fast
  • Quality control: catch and correct production problems quickly
  • Extra services: better tracking, customisation, self-service tools

Major logistics functions

Function Key decisions
Warehousing Storing finished goods until sold. Decide location, number, size, layout, building design and ownership. Automated warehouses cut injuries, labour costs and theft
Inventory management Balance too much (high carrying cost: finance, insurance, storage, losses) vs too little (stock-outs, emergency shipments, unhappy customers)
Transportation Choose among rail, truck, water, pipeline and air, based on cost, infrastructure and urgency (fastest is usually most expensive)
Order processing The purchase order is the central legal document: product, price, delivery, payment terms. Computerised systems speed up the order–shipping–billing cycle
Logistical packaging Protects against damage, eases handling and saves storage space. It's different from product packaging, which serves marketing goals
Information Logistics is information-based: identification, storage, analysis and decision support
Exam focus

  1. Its channel levels (e.g. manufacturer → distributor → wholesaler → retailer → consumer) and channel behaviour (conflict, VMS type)
  2. How it analyses consumer needs (the five service outputs)
  3. How it sets channel objectives (company, product, intermediaries, competitors, environment)
  4. Its channel alternatives (types and number of intermediaries, i.e. intensive distribution, and members' responsibilities)
