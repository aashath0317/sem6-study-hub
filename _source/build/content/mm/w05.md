## Pixel planes, points and line equations

A vector shape is stored mathematically, but a pixel display must choose discrete pixel locations to show it. **Rasterization** maps continuous geometric descriptions onto a pixel grid. Figure 5.1 shows a house made of lines and shapes; enlargement exposes the selected pixels. Raster drawing tools also need a way to choose the initial pixels for shapes.

A **point** is the fundamental building block. The application supplies a coordinate and the output device activates the appropriate location. The lesson describes a CRT electron beam illuminating phosphor as a historical example. A thicker point may cover several pixels. A **line algorithm** calculates intermediate positions between endpoints and chooses nearby pixels.

Rounding a real position such as (11.38, 21.59) gives pixel (11,22). Diagonal lines may therefore show stair steps called **jaggies**. More display resolution can help; anti-aliasing adjusts edge intensities rather than pretending that the grid is continuous.

A satisfactory line should look straight, start and end accurately without gaps between connected segments, maintain reasonably uniform visual thickness across lengths and slopes, and be generated quickly.

:::formula
Line: y = mx + c
Slope: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) = Δy/Δx
Intercept: c = y₁ − mx₁
Increment relation: Δy = mΔx
:::

For a vertical line, Δx is zero and this slope form is undefined. Plot its fixed x and step y instead. For a zero-length line, plot one point. An algorithm must also handle negative coordinates, negative slopes and reversed endpoint order.

## DDA: method and full iteration tables

**Digital Differential Analyzer (DDA)** is an incremental scan-conversion method. It avoids repeatedly multiplying in y = mx + c. The PDF chooses a unit step along the dominant coordinate and adds a fractional increment to the other coordinate.

For a positive shallow slope, Δx = 1 and y next = y + m. For a positive steep slope, Δy = 1 and x next = x + 1/m. Generalise safely using **steps = max(|Δx|, |Δy|)**, x increment = Δx/steps and y increment = Δy/steps. The signed increments preserve the endpoint direction. Keep the accumulated real values and round only the plotted coordinates.

:::warn Source ambiguity and rounding convention
The PDF's negative-slope summary omits the direction signs. Use signed increments. Its “first 3 points” example lists the starting point plus three updates. Here **first N points includes the starting point**; a full table also shows later points. Rounding ties here go away from zero. State a tie rule if an exam question does not supply one; different tie conventions can select different equally near pixels.
:::

DDA is simple and faster than direct substitution at every point. It still uses real-number arithmetic and rounding. Repeated floating-point additions can accumulate error on long lines. The PDF suggests separating integer and fractional parts as one route to integer implementation.

## Bresenham: derive the integer decision

For a shallow line advancing to the right, choose either E = (x+1,y) or NE = (x+1,y+1). The mathematical line passes between them. Let d₁ be the distance from the lower pixel to the line, and d₂ the distance from the line to the upper pixel.

Using y = m(x+1)+c gives d₁ − d₂ = 2m(x+1)+2c−2y−1. Multiply by positive Δx and substitute m = Δy/Δx. The sign is unchanged, but fractional division disappears. The decision can be written pᵢ = 2Δy xᵢ − 2Δx yᵢ + A, where A = 2Δy + Δx(2c−1). Subtract consecutive decisions to obtain p next = p + 2Δy − 2Δx(y next−y). Substituting the initial point gives **p₁ = 2Δy−Δx**.

:::formula
If p < 0: choose E; p next = p + 2Δy
Otherwise: choose NE; p next = p + 2Δy − 2Δx
:::

Plot the start, calculate the two increments once, and repeat for Δx transitions to reach the endpoint. For steep slopes, interchange the major and minor axes and swap them back for output. For negative slopes, apply the sign of the minor-axis change. The tables use positive major/minor distances for the decision, with signed coordinate movement.

:::warn Source arithmetic wording
Page 11's prose refers once to 2Δy−Δx as an increment; that is the **initial decision**. The diagonal update is 2Δy−2Δx, as the numbered algorithm correctly shows. At p = 0, follow the PDF's “otherwise” branch. “First 5 points” in the example is again followed by six plotted points including the start.
:::

## Circle drawing from the PDF's equations

All points of a circle are radius r from its centre (x꜀,y꜀). Figure 5.5 marks the centre, radius and angle.

:::formula
(x−x꜀)² + (y−y꜀)² = r²
x = x꜀ + r cos θ
y = y꜀ + r sin θ
:::

A circle has **eight-way symmetry**. For a centre-relative point (a,b), reflect/sign-change it to (±a,±b) and (±b,±a), then add the centre. Generate only one 45° octant. The PDF describes moving from 90° toward 45°, so x increases and y decreases. Pixels should approximate the true circle as closely as possible.

The PDF does **not** provide a midpoint/Bresenham circle decision recurrence. The worked method here is a direct application of its Cartesian equation: for each integer a ≥ 0, calculate b = √(r²−a²), round b for plotting, and stop before plotting any a > rounded b. Reflect the results. This is a clear equation-based sampling method, not a claimed midpoint algorithm.

## Vector objects, curves, conversions and filled areas

A **vector object** uses mathematical parameters defining position, size and direction. Tools provide lines, circles, ellipses, arcs and curves. Move, scale, rotate, mirror, stretch, skew or deform an object by changing its geometry. More complex artwork combines primitives. Set operations on closed shapes include union, difference and intersection. The final screen result still depends on display resolution.

A **Bézier curve** is a mathematically defined path. Nodes locate important points; control handles govern the curve's direction and shape around a node. Figure 5.7 shows three nodes, with handles visible at the selected middle node. Pen, curve and shape tools create or adjust these paths. Converting text or a basic shape to curves allows node-level editing, but text converted to curves no longer behaves as ordinary editable characters.

**Rasterization** converts vector objects or text into a bitmap at a chosen resolution. **Vectorization/tracing** attempts the reverse: it infers paths from pixels. A bitmap does not explicitly label “this is a circle”, so tracing involves approximation. Inspect boundaries, unnecessary nodes, holes and colour regions. A trace is not guaranteed to recreate the original geometry exactly.

**Filled areas** use a boundary and fill parameters. The software determines which region receives colour or a pattern. A closed shape gives an unambiguous interior. Vector fills and effects can involve more processing than a simple pixel fill; the PDF presents this as a general cost comparison, but actual effort depends on geometry, effects and implementation.

## The complete coffee-logo tutorial

The supplied logo demonstrates shapes, grouping, duplication, rotation, mirroring, fill, import, shadows and text on a path. The final arrangement has a decorative triangular background, circles, a central diamond, a cup and a curved banner.

1. Create a new drawing and zoom to width. Choose Smart drawing; set shape recognition Highest, smoothing Medium and outline width 1.0 pt. Draw a large triangle and a smaller triangle, position the smaller inside, select both and Group.
2. Enable Snap to objects. Rotate the group 45°. Duplicate it, set the duplicate offset if prompted, mirror horizontally, and place it beside the first with touching corners. Select both groups, duplicate, mirror vertically and position the copy below. This creates four paired triangular groups.
3. Draw a circle with Ellipse, holding Ctrl for a constrained circle in the tutorial. Position it between the triangle edges and duplicate until there are six circles in total. Place them around the background.
4. Ungroup for colouring. Select the four outer triangles and set RGB (153,153,51). Select the inner triangles and set RGB (255,204,0). Select the six circles and set RGB (255,249,116).

## Calculation practice and answer-it-yourself

### DDA: five practice questions

<details markdown="1"><summary>1. Find the first three DDA pixels for (0,0)→(4,2), including start.</summary>

Increments = (1,0.5). Real points (0,0), (1,0.5), (2,1). With ties away from zero: **(0,0), (1,1), (2,1)**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>2. Find all DDA pixels for (1,1)→(1,4).</summary>

Δx = 0, Δy = 3, steps = 3. Increments (0,1). **(1,1), (1,2), (1,3), (1,4)**. Do not divide by zero to find a slope.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>3. Find the first three pixels for (0,3)→(4,1).</summary>

Steps = 4; increments (1,−0.5). Real values (0,3), (1,2.5), (2,2). **(0,3), (1,3), (2,2)** under the stated tie rule.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>4. How many plotted pixels including both endpoints for (2,2)→(6,7)?</summary>

Steps = max(4,5) = 5 transitions. **6 plotted positions** including the initial point.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>5. What does DDA do for identical endpoints (−2,1) and (−2,1)?</summary>

Steps = 0. **Plot (−2,1) once**; do not calculate increments by dividing by zero.

</details>

### Bresenham: five practice questions

<details markdown="1"><summary>1. Find p₁ for (0,0)→(5,2).</summary>

p₁ = 2Δy−Δx = 2×2−5 = **−1**. The first move is E to (1,0).

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>2. If p = −1, Δy = 2 and Δx = 5, find the next decision after E.</summary>

p next = p+2Δy = −1+4 = **3**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>3. If p = 3, Δy = 2 and Δx = 5, find the next decision after NE.</summary>

p next = p+2Δy−2Δx = 3+4−10 = **−3**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>4. What happens at p = 0 for a positive shallow line?</summary>

Use the PDF's “otherwise” branch: choose **(x+1,y+1)** and add 2Δy−2Δx. This states the tie convention.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>5. How do you adapt the shallow algorithm to (0,0)→(2,5)?</summary>

Swap the major/minor axes. Major = 5, minor = 2; p₁ = 4−5 = **−1**. Advance y every step; the first next pixel is **(0,1)**.

</details>

### Circle drawing: five practice questions

<details markdown="1"><summary>1. For r = 5 and relative x = 3, find relative y on the upper circle.</summary>

y = √(r²−x²) = √(25−9) = √16 = **4 pixel units**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>2. Give the rightmost point for centre (2,−1), radius 5.</summary>

x = x꜀+r = 2+5 = 7; y = y꜀ = −1. **(7,−1)**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>3. Use θ = 90° for centre (1,2), radius 3.</summary>

x = 1+3 cos 90° = 1; y = 2+3 sin 90° = 5. **(1,5)**.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>4. How many unique symmetry points come from relative (0,5)?</summary>

Sign changes and swapping give **4** unique cardinal points. Several of the eight formal reflections coincide.

</details>

<details markdown="1"><summary>5. Why is tracing a rasterized circle not guaranteed to return its original circle object?</summary>

Pixels record sampled colours, not the original centre, radius or object identity. Tracing estimates paths and may produce an approximate many-node curve. **It cannot guarantee recovery of the original geometry.**

</details>
