## Why graphs?

**Graphs are discrete structures made of vertices and edges that connect them.** They model a huge range of problems:

- **Competition** between species in an ecological niche
- **Who influences whom** in an organisation
- **Round-robin tournament** outcomes
- **Acquaintanceships** between people and **links** between websites
- **Roadmaps** and **assigning jobs** to employees
- Whether a circuit fits on a **planar circuit board**
- Telling apart **chemical compounds** with the same formula but different structures
- Whether two computers are **connected** in a network

## The birth of graph theory — Königsberg

:::example The Königsberg bridge problem
- **Königsberg** (Prussia) lay on the Pregel river, which split the city into **four landmasses** (including the island of Kneiphopf).
- **Seven bridges** linked them.
- Residents asked: *can you leave home, cross **each bridge exactly once**, and return home?*
- **Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)** solved it. He turned each landmass into a **vertex** (A, B, C, D) and each bridge into an **edge**. His method is considered the **birth of graph theory**.
:::

:::tip Euler's answer: no
For a finite undirected graph whose non-isolated vertices are connected, a closed walk using every edge exactly once exists iff **every vertex has even degree** (you leave a landmass as often as you enter it).

In Königsberg the degrees are **A = 5, B = 3, C = 3, D = 3**, all odd, so it's **impossible**. Load the Königsberg preset in the graph lab below to see it.
:::

## What is a graph?

:::key Definition 1
A **graph G = (V, E)** consists of:

- **V**: a **non-empty set of vertices** (nodes)
- **E**: a set of **edges**

Each edge has **one or two vertices** associated with it, called its **endpoints**. An edge **connects** its endpoints.
:::

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Infinite graph** | **Infinite** vertex set (or infinitely many edges) |
| **Finite graph** | **Finite** vertex set **and** finite edge set. *This course uses finite graphs only* [[v3 01:24]] |
| **Multiple edges** | Two or more edges joining the **same pair** of vertices |
| **Multiplicity m** | When m different edges join the same unordered pair {u, v}, {u, v} is an **edge of multiplicity m** [[v3 06:43]] |
| **Loop** | An edge that **starts and ends at the same vertex** [[v3 05:20]] |

### Degree of a vertex and the handshaking theorem (examined: both 2023 papers)

:::key Degree
- The **degree** deg(v) of a vertex in an undirected graph is the **number of edges at v**. A **loop counts twice**.
- deg(v) = 0: an **isolated** vertex. deg(v) = 1: a **pendant** vertex.
- In a **simple** graph with n vertices, every degree is at most **n − 1** (v can join each other vertex once).
- In a **directed** graph, deg⁻(v) is the **in-degree** (arrows in) and deg⁺(v) the **out-degree** (arrows out).
:::

:::formula
**Handshaking theorem:** Σ deg(v) = **2|E|**
Each edge has two ends, so it adds 2 to the total degree.
Directed graphs: Σ deg⁻(v) = Σ deg⁺(v) = |E|
:::

:::key Corollary
An undirected graph has an **even number of vertices of odd degree**. (The total is even, so the odd degrees must pair up.)
:::

*Example:* a graph has 10 edges. Its degrees add up to 2 × 10 = **20**.

### Can a graph with these degrees exist?

Check in this order and stop at the first failure:

1. **Sum must be even** (handshaking). Any graph type.
2. **Simple graph:** no degree above **n − 1**, and **0 and n − 1 can't both appear** (a vertex joined to everyone means nobody is isolated).
3. **Simple graph, final test (Havel–Hakimi):** sort the degrees largest first, remove the largest d, subtract 1 from the next d degrees, and repeat. If you ever get a negative number, or need more vertices than are left, it's **impossible**; if everything becomes 0, it's **possible**, and the steps tell you how to draw it.
4. **Multigraph (no loops):** possible exactly when the sum is even and the **largest degree ≤ the sum of all the others**.
5. **Pseudograph (loops allowed):** possible exactly when the sum is even.

:::example Worked checks
| Degrees | Type asked | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2, 3, 3, 3, 5 | Simple, 5 vertices | **Impossible** | Maximum degree is 4 |
| 1, 1, 3 with five edges | Any | **Impossible** | Sum must be 2 × 5 = 10, but it is 5 |
| 1, 2, 3, 4 | Simple | **Impossible** | 4 vertices allow at most degree 3 |
| 1, 2, 3, 4 | Multigraph | **Possible** | Sum 10 is even and 4 ≤ 1 + 2 + 3 |
| 3, 3, 1, 1 | Simple | **Impossible** | Each degree-3 vertex joins all 3 others, so every vertex has degree ≥ 2 |
| 2, 2, 2, 3, 4, 6 | Any | **Impossible** | Sum 19 is odd |
| 2, 2, 2, 4, 4 | Simple | **Possible** | Two vertices joined to each other and to the other three |
:::

:::tip How to write "draw it or explain why not"
If it is possible, **draw it and label each vertex with its degree**. If it isn't, **name the rule it breaks with the numbers**, e.g. "the degree sum is 19, which is odd, but Σ deg(v) = 2|E| must be even".
:::

### The pigeonhole principle and the "two equal degrees" proof

:::key Pigeonhole principle
If **k + 1 or more objects** go into **k boxes**, at least one box holds **two or more** objects.
:::

:::example Proof (both 2023 papers): every simple graph with n ≥ 2 vertices has two vertices of equal degree
1. Let G be a simple graph with n ≥ 2 vertices. Each degree is one of the **n values 0, 1, …, n − 1**.
2. **Case 1:** some vertex has degree **n − 1**. It is joined to every other vertex, so **no vertex has degree 0**. The degrees lie in {1, …, n − 1}: **n − 1 values**.
3. **Case 2:** no vertex has degree n − 1. The degrees lie in {0, …, n − 2}: again **n − 1 values**.
4. In both cases, **n vertices** (pigeons) share at most **n − 1 degree values** (boxes).
5. By the pigeonhole principle, **two vertices have the same degree**. ∎

Marks go to: the range of degrees, the reason 0 and n − 1 can't both appear, and naming the pigeonhole principle.
:::

## The six types of graphs

The PDF builds these types using a **computer network of data centres**: San Francisco, Los Angeles, Denver, Chicago, Detroit, New York, Washington.

### Undirected graphs

| Type | Definition | Network example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple graph** | Each edge connects **two different vertices**, and **no two edges** connect the same pair. **No multiple edges, no loops** | One link between each pair of connected data centres (Figure 3). Each edge can be named by its unordered pair {u, v} |
| **Multigraph** | **Multiple edges allowed**, **no loops** [[v3 06:09]] | Several links between the same data centres, e.g. two between Los Angeles and Denver (Figure 4) |
| **Pseudograph** | **Multiple edges and loops** allowed [[v4 00:01]] | A link from a data centre to itself, a **diagnostic feedback loop** (Figure 5). Not every vertex needs a loop |

### Directed graphs (digraphs)

Some links work in **one direction only** (simplex links), e.g. heavy traffic one way. So we use **directed edges (arcs)**, drawn as **arrows** [[v4 01:42]].

:::key Definition 2 — directed graph
A **directed graph (digraph) G = (V, E)** has a non-empty vertex set V and a set E of **directed edges**. Each directed edge is associated with an **ordered pair (u, v)**: it **starts at u and ends at v**.
:::

:::tip Ordered vs unordered pairs [[v4 03:06]]
- **{u, v}** (undirected): you can travel u → v **or** v → u.
- **(u, v)** (directed): you must start at **u** and end at **v**. San Francisco → Denver does **not** allow Denver → San Francisco.
:::

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Simple directed graph** | Directed, **no loops, no multiple directed edges**. At most one edge per ordered pair, so (u, v) names the edge |
| **Directed multigraph** | Directed, **multiple directed edges** (and loops) allowed. Formally G = (V, E, f), where f maps each edge to an ordered pair (Figure 7: e₁ v₁→v₂, e₂ v₂→v₃, e₄ v₁→v₃) |
| **Mixed graph** | Has **both directed and undirected** edges, e.g. a network with two-way and one-way links |

### Table 1 — graph terminology (learn this)

| Type | Edges | Multiple edges? | Loops? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple graph | Undirected | No | No |
| Multigraph | Undirected | **Yes** | No |
| Pseudograph | Undirected | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Simple directed graph | **Directed** | No | No |
| Directed multigraph | **Directed** | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Mixed graph | **Directed and undirected** | **Yes** | **Yes** |

:::tip How to classify any graph [[v5 00:50]]
Ask three questions, in order:

1. **Arrows?** None means undirected; all means directed; some means **mixed**.
2. **Multiple edges** between the same pair?
3. **Loops?**

Then read the type off Table 1.

## Graph models

When building a model, answer the three key questions: **directed or undirected? multiple edges? loops?**

| Model | Vertex | Edge | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acquaintanceship graph** (social network) | A person | **Undirected**: the two people **know each other** | **Simple graph**: no multiple edges, usually no loops (loops only if you model "self-knowledge"). The lecture gives historical illustrative estimates of about 7 billion vertices and over a trillion edges; these are not current measured counts [[v6 01:03]] |
| **Influence graph** (social network) | A person | **Directed a → b**: a **influences** b | **Simple directed graph** (no loops or multiple edges). E.g. Deborah influences Brian, Fred and Linda, but no one influences her; Yvonne and Brian influence each other [[v6 03:16]] |
| **Web graph** (information network) | A web page | **Directed a → b** if page a **links to** b | Directed. The lecture gives historical figures of **3 billion vertices and 20 billion edges**; these are not current measured web counts [[v6 04:39]] |
| **Citation graph** (information network) | A paper or patent | Directed: a **cites** b | Directed, no loops or multiple edges |
| **Roadmap graph** | An **intersection** | A **road**: undirected = two-way, directed = one-way, multiple = several roads, loop = loop road | Two-way only, no loops or duplicates: **simple**. One-way only: **simple directed**. Both: **mixed** |
| **Airline routes** | An airport | A **directed edge for each flight** | **Directed multigraph** [[v6 06:40]] |
| **Round-robin tournament** | A team | **(a, b)** if team **a beats b** | **Simple directed graph** (each pair plays once). In Figure 10, Team 1 is undefeated and Team 3 is winless [[v6 07:38]] |
| **Single-elimination tournament** (extra) | A game | Directed from a game to the next game | Directed (tree-like) |

:::example The airline exercise, three ways [[v5 02:20]]
Cities: Boston, Newark, Miami, Detroit, Washington.

- **(a)** One edge between cities that have **any** flight between them → **simple graph**.
- **(b)** One edge **for each flight** → **multigraph** (several flights between the same cities).
- **(c)** The same as (b) **plus a loop** for a sightseeing trip that takes off and lands in Miami → **pseudograph**.
:::

**Graph types in one table (memorise):**

| Type | Loops (edge from a vertex to itself)? | Several edges between the same 2 vertices? | Edge direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple graph** | No | No | Undirected |
| **Multigraph** | No | **Yes** | Undirected |
| **Pseudograph** | **Yes** | Yes | Undirected |
| **Directed graph** | Yes | No (one per direction) | **Directed** |
| **Mixed graph** | Yes | Yes | **Both** directed and undirected |

**Q1.** Which graph has each edge joining two **different** vertices and **no two edges** joining the same pair?

(a) simple (b) regular (c) pseudograph (d) directed

**✅ Answer: (a) simple graph**

“Two different vertices” = no loops; “no two edges on the same pair” = no multiple edges → **simple graph**.
(Regular = every vertex has the same degree — a different idea.)


**Q2.** A graph with both directed and undirected edges is a:

(a) directed multigraph (b) mixed graph (c) directed graph (d) simple directed graph

**✅ Answer: (b) mixed graph**

Mixed = a **mix** of arrows and plain lines.


**Q3.** People who can influence the thinking of others are modelled by an ______.

(a) roadmap graph (b) acquaintanceship graph (c) web graph (d) influence graph

**✅ Answer: (d) influence graph**

**Influence graph:** a directed edge a → b means “a influences b” (direction matters).
**Acquaintanceship graph:** an undirected edge means two people know each other.


**Q4.** A highway system: cities are vertices, with an edge if there is an interstate highway between them. Which graph?

(a) multigraph (b) simple graph (c) directed graph (d) pseudograph

**✅ Answer: (b) simple graph**

The question only asks **whether** a highway exists between two cities (yes/no), not how many, and roads go both ways → undirected, at most one edge per pair → **simple graph**.
(If it counted *each* highway separately it would be a multigraph.)


**Q5.** A round-robin tournament can be modelled using a ______.

(a) directed graph (b) mixed graph (c) multigraph (d) simple graph

**✅ Answer marked by the quiz: (c) multigraph**

The quiz accepted **multigraph**. Note: the Rosen textbook models a round-robin tournament as a **directed graph** (an edge a → b means team a beat team b). For this MCQ, pick the quiz's answer; in a written answer, explain the model you use.


**Q6.** Which property does a simple graph **not** need to have?

(a) no loops or multiple edges (b) must be unweighted (c) must be connected (d) all of the above

**✅ Answer: (c) must be connected**

A simple graph only forbids loops and multiple edges. It can be in several separate pieces (disconnected) and still be simple.


**Q7.** Which is true?

(a) no edges and no vertices (b) no edges and many vertices (c) none (d) many edges and no vertices

**✅ Answer marked by the quiz: (d) — but be careful**

The quiz key marks (d). Logically, though, **an edge must join two vertices**, so a graph cannot have edges without vertices. The statement that is really true is **(b)**: a graph can have vertices and **no** edges (an *empty* or *null* graph).
For the MCQ, remember the quiz marked (d); for a written answer, use the correct reasoning: every edge needs endpoints.


**Q8.** G has v vertices and e edges, is **connected** and has **no cycles**. Which is true?

(a) v = e (b) none (c) v + 1 = e (d) v = e + 1

**✅ Answer: (d) v = e + 1**

“Connected with no cycles” is the definition of a **tree**. **A tree with v vertices has exactly v − 1 edges**, so e = v − 1 → v = e + 1.
Check: 3 vertices in a line A–B–C have 2 edges ✓.
