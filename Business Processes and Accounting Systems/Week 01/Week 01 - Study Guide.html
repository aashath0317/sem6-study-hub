1Business processes

Definition

A business process is a collection of related, structured activities or tasks, done by people or equipment in a specific sequence, that produce a service or product (serving a business goal) for customers.

  • Business processes happen at all organisational levels, and may or may not be visible to customers.
  • They can be modelled as a flowchart (activities with decision points) or a process matrix (activities with rules based on data).
  • Benefits: improved customer satisfaction and agility in reacting to rapid market change.

The three core business processes (Bonnie K. Klamm, 2004)

  1. Acquire & pay for resources
  1. Convert resources into goods/services
  1. Sell goods/services & collect revenue
Process What happens Example transactions
1. Acquire and pay for resources Buy what the business needs and pay for it Raw materials, labour, electricity; purchases, accounts payable, payroll
2. Convert resources Turn inputs into goods or services Production, inventory movements
3. Sell and collect Sell goods or services and collect revenue Sales orders, invoices, accounts receivable, cash receipts

Businesses need information about these processes to make operational decisions, improve efficiency and generate financial reports.

Porter's value chain

Michael Porter's value chain links these processes. Each has financial and non-financial activities that create value for customers.

  • Primary activities: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service.
  • Support activities: firm infrastructure (including accounting), HR, technology, procurement.

Typical financial transaction areas:

  • purchasing, production, marketing, personnel
  • accounts payable, accounts receivable
  • fixed assets, inventory

A large organisation has thousands of these activities, which is why a computerised accounting system is important.

2Accounting information and its users

Accounting information

Information with a monetary association (John F. Nash, 1993), expressed in figures, ratios, percentages or units. It covers transactions with vendors, customers and employees, and is generated by economic events and transactions.

Internal users External users
Management, employees, shareholders (owners) Vendors (suppliers), potential investors, government authorities (tax, regulators), lenders and banks, customers

3What is an Accounting Information System?

Definitions
  • Lecture notes: an AIS is a delivery system for accounting information. It collects, stores, manages, processes, retrieves and reports the financial data generated by business processes.
  • Video (Fundamental Accounting Principles, 22nd ed.): "people, records and methods that collect and process data from transactions and events, organise them in useful forms, and communicate results to decision makers" 00:00.

The AIS is the whole process that turns raw transactions into useful information, usually the financial statements 00:31. Designing one means asking: computerised or manual? Who collects the data? How is it compiled?

Why AIS matters

  1. Meet statutory reporting requirements (e.g. annual financial statements, tax returns).
  2. Make reliable financial data available to the parties who need it.
  3. Keep data intact and secure.

Principles to consider when designing an AIS 00:58

Principle Meaning
Control Managers must be able to control and monitor operations: internal controls protect assets and data
Relevance Report useful, understandable, timely information for decisions
Compatibility The system must fit the organisation's activities, people and structure
Flexibility Able to adapt to changes: growth, new regulations, new products
Cost–benefit Benefits must outweigh the costs of the system

4The five components of a computerised AIS

Past systems were paper-based and manual. Today almost all are computerised. A computerised AIS has five components (Stephen A. Moscove, 2001):

Component Description
1. People Provide input, monitor processing, interpret output: accountants, auditors, financial analysts, consultants
2. Procedures and instructions The methods for collecting, storing, retrieving, processing data, manual or automated. Today they're coded into the AIS software; staff learn them through documentation and training; they must be followed consistently
3. Data All financial transactions and events: anything that affects the company's finances. Examples: sales orders, customer billing statements, purchase requisitions, vendor invoices, inventory data, payroll, tax. Data must be complete, correct and relevant. Needs a database structure, input screens for different users, and output formats for different needs
4. Software Programs to store, retrieve, process and analyse financial data. Must have quality, reliability and security
5. Hardware Input, processing and output devices: computers, mobile devices, servers, printers, surge protectors, routers, storage media, backup power

Size Software

Four ways to obtain AIS software:

  1. Buy off-the-shelf software.
  2. Customise existing software for unique needs.
  3. Develop in-house.
  4. Have a third party develop it specifically for the organisation.

Hardware purchase considerations:

  • compatibility with the software
  • cost, speed, storage capacity
  • whether it can be expanded and upgraded

5Internal control & security

An AIS holds the organisation's financial resources, so it must be protected from loss, waste and theft. Internal control ensures reliable information and promotes operational efficiency.

Objectives of internal control (Moscove, 2001)
  1. Safeguarding assets
  2. Checking the accuracy and reliability of accounting data
  3. Promoting operational efficiency
  4. Encouraging adherence to prescribed managerial policies
Everyday internal controls
  • Segregation of duties: the person who approves payments doesn't also record them.
  • Passwords and user rights in the AIS software.
  • Regular backups and bank reconciliations.
  • Pre-numbered invoices and receipts.
Exam focus

Expect:

  • Define a business process and list the three core processes
  • Define accounting information and its users
  • Define AIS and explain its importance
