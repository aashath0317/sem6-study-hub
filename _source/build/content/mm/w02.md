## Stages and the improvement cycle

A multimedia title needs an engineered process. It brings together different activities, people, tools and assets. Producing media takes time, so a clear plan reduces expensive rework. The process must serve the target audience rather than simply show what the software can do.

:::flow Four broad stages in the PDF
Planning and costing
Designing and producing
Testing
Delivering
:::

These stages are connected. Testing can reveal that the design needs a change. Feedback after delivery can start another planning cycle. A process diagram describes the work; it does not mean that nobody may revisit an earlier decision.

:::warn ADDIE wording in the source
The paragraph on page 2 says “ADDIEA” and lists Analyze, Design, Develop, Integrate, Evaluate and Analyze. Its Figure 2.1 instead labels **Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation and Evaluation**. Use these five labels when explaining the figure, and explain that evaluation feeds improvement. The inconsistency is in the PDF.
:::

| Stage | Main question | Useful output |
|---|---|---|
| Planning and costing | Why, for whom, with which resources? | Goals, audience profile, scope, budget and specification |
| Designing and producing | How will it look, flow and operate? | Outline, navigation map, storyboard, script and assets |
| Testing | Does it work and meet its purpose? | Recorded faults, feedback and verified corrections |
| Delivering | How will users obtain and run it? | Packaged title, installation steps and troubleshooting |

## Planning, requirements and cost

An idea may come from an individual or a group. **Brainstorming** aims to generate many ideas before analysing which ones fit the purpose and constraints. A good idea still needs a defined audience, treatment and practical specification.

1. **Develop the concept:** explain the vision and message.
2. **State goals and objectives:** say what the title should achieve.
3. **Identify the audience:** describe who will use it.
4. **Determine the treatment:** decide how to present the idea, such as a guided lesson or explorable story.
5. **Develop specifications:** state what the title includes and how it must operate.
6. **Plan storyboard and navigation:** show the screens and their connections.

**Scope** means the boundary of the project. Specify included content, excluded content, development time, required skills and application organisation. Without a boundary, every new idea can become another task. Scope helps balance ambition against available resources.

Research these audience factors: age group, existing knowledge or education, technological background, language, gender, economic background and profession. Use the findings to design appropriately; do not assume that every person in a demographic group has identical needs. A schoolchild, a tourist and an experienced technician may need different vocabulary and navigation.

**Content analysis** determines the breadth and depth of information. Decide what can be reused, what must be created, what needs permission, and what must be digitised. Capturing photographs, audio and video and creating text, graphics, sound effects and animation all require time and skills. Balance the content with the audience's expectations.

The PDF identifies **time, money and people** as three changing elements in an estimate. More content or complex interaction can require more production effort. Plan specialist responsibilities and review points before promising a delivery date.

| Team role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Project manager | Leads development and implementation, coordinates daily work, and combines management with technical understanding |
| Multimedia designer | Designs visuals, illustration, animation, interface, storyboards and clear, consistent information flow |
| Video specialist | Shoots, captures and edits video |
| Audio specialist | Records speech, produces music, narration and sound effects |
| Multimedia programmer | Integrates the assets with an authoring system or programming language into a functioning title |

## Activity 2.1: a complete title plan

**Question:** Plan a multimedia title with an idea, goal and objectives, target audience, delivery and playback medium, team roles, tools and equipment.

:::example Model answer: “Read a Sri Lankan Bus Route”
**Idea:** a short interactive learning title showing how to choose the correct bus and recognise a route number. **Goal:** help first-time travellers use route information confidently. **Objectives:** learners identify the origin and destination, choose a suitable route from a simple map, and explain where to check a route number.

**Audience:** new university students who use a phone, have limited local travel knowledge, and may prefer Tamil, Sinhala or English. Provide simple language, readable text and replayable narration.

**Delivery and play:** a lightweight website playable in a phone browser. Store essential text and diagrams locally for a downloadable version. Compress optional video and give a text equivalent for limited connections.

**Roles:** the project manager controls scope and reviews; the designer makes maps and screens; the video specialist records a demonstration; the audio specialist records narration; the programmer connects the screens and feedback. A transport-informed reviewer checks accuracy.

**Tools and equipment:** a phone camera and microphone for capture, a computer for processing, an image/vector editor for the route map, audio and video editors, a web authoring environment, test phones, storage and a server for distribution.

**Scope and checks:** cover one fictional training route rather than promise a national live timetable. Test menu choices, map labels, narration controls and feedback with actual new students before release. Check that language choices and low-bandwidth use work.
:::

## Design principles and the detailed process

Multimedia design coordinates several media assets into a meaningful presentation. Apply the principles to the message, rather than adding effects everywhere.

| Principle | Meaning | Application to the bus lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Balance | Distribute visual weight so no area unintentionally overpowers the rest | Balance the map and instruction panel |
| Harmony | Blend elements into an integrated whole | Use a consistent visual and narration style |
| Emphasis | Attract attention to an important element | Highlight the destination before asking a question |
| Movement | Guide the viewer's eye through the layout | Lead from the route label to the map and answer |
| Rhythm or pattern | Repeat elements to create an organised pattern | Repeat lesson headings and navigation positions |
| Contrast | Use purposeful differences for interest and emphasis | Distinguish the selected route from other routes |
| Hierarchy or proportion | Arrange elements by importance and give suitable emphasis | Make the main heading stronger than supporting detail |

Balance does not require every element to be identical. Emphasis would be impossible if every element had equal visual priority. The source's “equal emphasis” wording should be understood as balanced visual weight.

After requirements and goals, establish a **content definition** and **content outline**. Define the logical flow, map navigation and develop the storyboard. Figure 2.2 places requirements and goals at the top; content definition, content outlining and logical-flow/navigation planning below; asset capture and storyboarding next; and implementation at the bottom. It distinguishes client work, shared client/developer work and developer work.

Asset capture + storyboard, agreed with the client
Implementation by the development team

## Storyboard, navigation map and script

A **storyboard** organises ideas about a presentation's form. For a video it shows shots that form a scene. For an application it sketches screens, their media and transitions. It begins with the project's goals and helps turn them into an initial specification. Figure 2.3 shows screen sketches for a college title: welcome, main menu, information, courses, registration and services.

A **navigation map** shows links between scenes or content components. It can be combined with the logical-flow definition or supplied separately. Figure 2.4 adds routes between the college screens; the “Courses” screen branches into credit and non-credit information. A storyboard answers “what is on each screen?” A map answers “how can users move between screens?”

A **script** supplies detailed textual instructions. It may include layout sketches, narration and dialogue. It should make the intended experience clear enough that another team member can implement it.

- Divide it into small screens and number the sequence.
- State every media element and interaction explicitly.
- Use an enthusiastic conversational tone and active sentences.
- Give feedback for both correct and incorrect exercise answers.
- Explain how to attract audience attention.
- Design the user's actions and the application's responses.

:::example One scripted screen
**Screen R03, route selection.** Show a simplified map on the left and a question on the right. Narration: “Choose the bus travelling to the library.” The user selects a route label. A correct answer highlights the destination and explains the match. An incorrect answer identifies that route's destination and invites another attempt. Include Home, Back and Next controls with explicit destinations.
:::

## Production, hardware and authoring metaphors

Implementation follows the storyboard and script. Identify and capture or create the assets before integrating them. **Authoring** orchestrates prepared text, images, audio, animation and video as one coherent title. Large media may remain external linked files controlled by the application. Test those dependencies when packaging; placing a file on the developer's computer is not enough.

Capturing devices obtain media; processing devices edit and encode it; playing devices present it; distribution devices deliver or copy it. Examples are cameras and microphones, computers, displays and speakers, and servers, networks or disc-copying equipment. Multimedia can demand high performance because of the volume, density, speed and variety of its data.

Authoring programs can supply drawing, text and animation tools, screen templates, hyperlinks and interaction. The PDF describes these metaphors, or ways of organising authoring work:

| Metaphor | Organisation and example |
|---|---|
| Scripting language | Commands implement conditions, jumps, loops and functions; ActionScript, Lingo and AWScripts are lesson examples |
| Slide show | Sequential slides with possible jumps; PowerPoint |
| Hierarchical | User choices are arranged in a tree, often menus |
| Iconic or flow-control | Toolbox icons form a flowchart; Authorware |
| Frames | Links between icons express conceptual relationships rather than only execution flow |
| Card and scripting | Linked index-card structure for hypertext/hypermedia; HyperCard and SuperCard |
| Cast, score and scripting | Time runs horizontally; tracks hold media instances from a cast; event procedures control behaviour; Director with Lingo |
| Timeline-based | Synchronises text, graphics, animation, video and sound over time; the lesson also describes scripting control of external playback equipment |

These software names reflect the course's historical examples. Learn the metaphor and choose a tool that fits the required interaction and delivery platform.

## Testing, delivery and review answers

Testing checks accuracy, functionality, appearance and achievement of the objective. Evaluate every feature, action button and link, and run the title on different hardware and under different conditions. Record enough detail to reproduce a fault, correct it, then repeat the relevant check.

| Alpha testing | Beta testing |
|---|---|
| Earlier in implementation; content or features may be incomplete | Near final release; title should function fully |
| Often the team, selected users or friends | Real target users outside the team |
| Reviews concept, format, interface and layout | Finds remaining defects and content problems |
| Guides early improvements | Detailed reports guide final fixes |

Delivery or deployment must match the planned medium. Arrange required hardware, software, plugins and installation. Provide numbered installation and startup instructions, likely problems and solutions. Check the delivered copy, including linked files, rather than only the development copy.

**Review question 1: Describe differences in development approaches by title type.** An educational title starts with learning objectives, suitable explanations, learner control and feedback. An entertainment title emphasises story, timing, immersion and responsive interaction. A linear film needs a shot storyboard and timed script; an interactive title additionally needs navigation routes, event responses and branching tests. All still require planning, design/production, testing and delivery, with effort adapted to the assets and audience.

**Review question 2: Describe authoring software for education and entertainment.** A slide tool can create a guided lesson with hyperlinks; card or hierarchical authoring supports selectable topics; scripting supports answer checking and game rules; timeline or cast/score authoring coordinates narration, animation and video. PowerPoint, HyperCard/SuperCard, Authorware and Director are the PDF examples. Explain each one's metaphor and suitability rather than merely listing names.

:::exam English answer frame
“First analyse the purpose, audience, content and scope. Estimate time, cost and team roles. Define the outline, logical flow, navigation map, storyboard and script. Capture or create the assets and integrate them with suitable authoring software. Conduct alpha and beta testing, correct defects, and deliver a complete package with installation and troubleshooting instructions. Use evaluation feedback for improvement.”
:::
