## Mental models

:::key Definition
A **mental model** is the **internal explanation a person builds to simulate how a system works**. It lets people **anticipate consequences, predict system states and plan actions** before touching the device.
:::

:::example The bus bell rope
A passenger pulls the overhead bell rope and expects a **ding**. If it doesn't ring, there are two possibilities:

1. **Mechanical breakage**: the user's **mental model is correct**, but the **physical implementation** has failed.
2. **Silent change to electronic push buttons**: the rope was disconnected **without clear signifiers** pointing passengers to the new seat buttons. The old mental model no longer applies, so passengers are **confused and frustrated**.
:::

Great design either **matches the users' intuitive expectations** or **naturally teaches** a new model through use.

## Representations

A **representation** is the **visual, structural and interactive presentation** of the system given to the user.

- It is **not** a raw reflection of the code or database (the **implementation model**).
- It's a **designed projection** that matches the **user's expected mental model**.

:::flow
Implementation model (code, DB)
Representation (UI layer)
User's mental model
:::

## Four traits of good representations

| # | Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Make relationships explicit** | Put **conceptually linked elements close together** to reduce searching and help decisions | Price shown right beside "Add to cart" |
| 2 | **Bring objects and actions together** | Put controls **right next to the object they act on**, not in a far-off toolbar | A delete ✕ on each list row |
| 3 | **Exclude extraneous details** | Strip out technical internals, backend codes and clutter | "Payment failed: card expired", not "ERR_0x8004" |
| 4 | **Expose natural constraints** | Make invalid actions **physically or visually impossible** | A date picker that blocks invalid days |

### Principles that support accurate mental models [[v3 00:01]]

| Principle | Why it helps mental models |
|---|---|
| **Consistency** | People are **biased towards past experience**. They expect a new interface to work the way they've done things before |
| **Affordance** | The interface should **teach the user how to use it** [[v3 00:50]] |
| **Mapping** | A good representation lets users **predict the outcome** of an action, so map interface elements to user actions [[v3 01:18]] |

## Full error taxonomy: slips vs mistakes

Week 2 introduced slips vs mistakes. Norman divides them further [[v4 00:14]]:

| Category | Sub-type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SLIP** (right goal, wrong execution) | ⚡ **Action-based** | The user **knows the right action** but **performs the wrong action**, or the **right action on the wrong object** [[v4 01:31]] | Pressing the light switch **next to** the one you meant [[v4 02:48]]; clicking the **middle touchpad button** (scroller) instead of right-click [[v4 03:50]] |
| | ⏳ **Memory-lapse** | Knows the command but **forgets a step** in the immediate sequence [[v4 04:47]] | Pressing F8 instead of F5 to refresh; forgetting to click "Update" when attaching |
| **MISTAKE** (wrong goal or plan) | 📜 **Rule-based** | **Correctly assesses** the situation but applies the **wrong rule or decision** [[v4 05:42]] | Knows the car is skidding but brakes hard (wrong rule) |
| | 🧠 **Knowledge-based** | **Assesses wrongly** because of **insufficient knowledge**, giving a faulty mental model [[v4 06:51]] | Thinks closing the laptop lid saves the document |
| | 🔄 **Memory-lapse** | **Forgets the entire goal or action cycle**, often after an interruption | Opens the browser to pay a bill, gets distracted, closes it with the bill unpaid |

:::warn Memory-lapse slip vs memory-lapse mistake [[v4 06:51]]
- **Slip:** you **know** what to do but **forgot one step at that moment**.
- **Mistake:** you've **forgotten the whole action cycle**, so you don't know how to get around.
:::

:::tip Severity
**Slips are less serious**: the intention was right, so **improving the presentation** usually fixes them. **Mistakes need deeper fixes**, like training or a better conceptual model. Rule-based mistakes in particular must be looked at seriously [[v4 05:42]].
:::

## Learned helplessness & expert blindness

A good designer must avoid **two traps** [[v5 00:50]]:

:::grid
**😞 Learned helplessness**

**Repeated failures** on badly designed interfaces make users believe **they personally can't handle the technology**, so they **give up learning**.

After continuous failure there's **no faith left**. Even a new, improved method won't win them back [[v5 01:53]].

**Cure:**
- Forgiving interfaces
- Clear success indicators
- Non-judgemental error recovery
- **Provision for people who learn differently and at different speeds**, which is why systems have **accessibility options** [[v5 01:53]]
+++
**🙈 Expert blindness**

Designers become **so familiar** with a tool that the **intermediate steps become invisible** to them, so they **leave out key cues** for novices [[v5 02:40]].

**Analogy:** giving directions to your house and forgetting key turns because they seem "obvious" to you.

**Cure:** **test with real novices** to expose hidden assumptions and missing signposts.
:::
