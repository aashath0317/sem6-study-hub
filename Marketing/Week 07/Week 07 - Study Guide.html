1Why marketing information matters

  • Gut feeling is not enough. You need sound, reliable, objective data to reduce risk and raise the chance of success.
  • Marketing research is collecting and analysing data about your target market, so you can make informed decisions through business intelligence.

Uses of marketing research: marketing plans, launching new products, exploring new markets, fine-tuning existing products, ad campaigns, pricing, choosing business locations.

Quality of information → quality of decisions

"Firms have to manage by facts and evidence, not gut feel or opinion." How a firm handles marketing information is the difference between winning and losing. Business success needs a sustainable, cutting-edge knowledge base (knowledge management).

Three developments increase the need for information:

  1. From local → national → global markets
  2. From buyer needs → wants
  3. From price → non-price competition

Marketing information is any marketing-related data that is readily useful to decision makers in a given situation.

Benefits of marketing information

Area Benefits
Planning Tap opportunities, build defences against threats, spot changing trends quickly, get market intelligence
Implementation Deliver customer-oriented 4Ps, better campaigns (target audience, media choice), build customer relationships (relationships rest on customer knowledge)
Control Raises the quality of all marketing decisions, which is the essence of effective control

2Three types of marketing data

Type What it is Examples
Internal data Information the company collects about customers and prospects during its own operations Leads, segmentation data (location, gender, age, behaviour), web traffic, sales patterns, billing (spend, timing), support tickets, satisfaction. Increasingly held in shared databases: enterprise systems and CRM
Competitive intelligence Information to understand competitors and market dynamics Product info, market share and penetration, pricing strategy, positioning and messaging (including the search keywords they dominate), win/loss analysis. Sources: competitor websites, social media, press releases, industry newsletters, blogs, conferences
Marketing research A systematic process for identifying opportunities and solving problems using customer insights from collected and analysed information Surveys, focus groups, experiments
Database marketing (marketing analytics)

Large databases combine demographics, in-store and online purchase history, search terms, page views and social posts. Data mining algorithms find patterns and recommend ways to increase sales, e.g. which behaviours predict a high lifetime value. This links to the Data Mining module!

3The marketing information system (MIS)

Definition

A marketing information system consists of people, equipment and procedures to gather, sort, analyse, evaluate and distribute needed, timely and accurate information to marketing decision makers.

It has four components (Kotler, 1998):

  1. Internal records system
  1. Marketing intelligence system
  1. Marketing research system
  1. Marketing decision support system (MDSS)
Component What it does
1. Internal records Basic data from daily operations. (a) The order-to-remittance cycle is the heart of the system: orders → documents → data. (b) Sales reporting system: up-to-date sales reports, automated in online systems. (c) Databases, data warehouses, data mining
2. Marketing intelligence Everyday information about developments in the environment. Managers scan in 4 ways: undirected viewing (general exposure, no purpose) · conditioned viewing (directed exposure, no active search) · informal search (limited, unstructured effort for specific information) · formal search (deliberate, planned methodology)
3. Marketing research Systematic design, collection, analysis and reporting of data on a specific marketing situation: a market survey, product preference test, regional sales forecast, advertising effectiveness study
4. MDSS A coordinated collection of data, systems, tools and techniques with software and hardware to interpret information and turn it into a basis for marketing action

Suppliers of marketing research

Type What they do
Syndicated-service firms Gather periodic consumer and trade data and sell it to many clients (e.g. Nielsen)
Custom research firms Carry out specific projects for one client; the report belongs to the client
Specialty-line firms Provide specialised services to other research firms, e.g. field service (interviewing)

Common research subjects:

  • environmental factors (economy, law, trends, technology, culture)
  • customer attitudes, behaviours and perceptions
  • product research (gaps, concept testing, market sizing, feature priorities, pricing, naming, positioning)
  • marketing, advertising and promotion research (segmentation, messages, media, sponsorships, packaging)
  • corporate research (reputation, brand building, M&A, partnerships)

Research is wise when a decision involves a significant change in direction. Projects range from a few hours in-house to over a year at a cost of hundreds of thousands.

4Types of marketing research

By objective

Type Purpose Methods / example
Exploratory Little is known: gain insights, clarify concepts, formulate the problem and hypotheses, eliminate impractical ideas Secondary data, observation, expert consultation, feedback
Descriptive Test hypotheses: describe who, what, how many. Measures frequencies and correlations Who are the buyers? How is it consumed? What % use it? Demand forecast?
Causal Establish cause and effect between variables If we change the packaging, what happens to durability or sales? (experiments)
Predictive Forecast market variables Future sales, growth projections, test markets for a new product

By type of data

🗣 Qualitative research

Feelings, attitudes, opinions, thoughts: the why behind behaviour. Small samples, depth and insight.

  • Focus group: 6–12 people discuss a topic, led by a moderator
  • Depth interview: extensive one-on-one, open-ended questions
  • Photo-ethnography: photographing behaviours and emotions instead of asking

🔢 Quantitative research

Hard facts and statistics: how many, how often, what proportion. Large samples, statistical significance.

  • Telephone surveys: a representative sample, closed-ended (yes/no) answers
  • Personal interviews: at a central location or at home; open and closed questions
  • Web surveys: emailed questionnaires to large groups, anytime and anywhere
  • Hybrid: two or more methods combined

5The marketing research process

  1. Define the problem & research objectives
  1. Develop the research plan & collect data
  1. Analyse the information
  1. Present the findings

1. Define the problem and objectives

  • Convert the management (decision) problem into a research problem.
  • "A well-defined problem is half solved." Define it neither too narrowly nor too broadly.
  • State the research objectives as research questions.

2. Develop the research plan and collect data

The plan covers five decisions:

Decision Options
A. Data source Primary data: freshly gathered for this specific purpose. Secondary data: already exists, gathered for another purpose
B. Research approach Observational, focus-group, survey, behavioural data, experimental
C. Research instrument Questionnaire (structure, question types, wording) · psychological tools (laddering, depth interviews) · mechanical devices (eye trackers, meters)
D. Sampling plan Sampling unit (who?) · sample size (how many?) · sampling procedure (how chosen?)
E. Contact method Mail questionnaire, personal interview, arranged interview, online
Careful: the PDF swaps primary and secondary data

The lesson PDF's definitions are reversed. The correct ones are:

  • Primary data is collected fresh for the current project (your own survey).
  • Secondary data already exists, collected for another purpose (census reports, company records, published studies).

3. Analyse the information

Tabulate the data, build frequency distributions, compute averages and measures of dispersion for the major variables, and interpret the findings.

4. Present the findings

Present the information to the relevant decision makers so they can make the right decisions.

Interpret the findings carefully 26:20. Wrong interpretation leads to wrong decisions, so marketers must find the insight, the message in the findings, and present it convincingly to management.

Same finding, two interpretations 26:53

A footwear company sends two salespeople to an African market to see whether there's demand for shoes. Both observe the same thing: nobody wears shoes there.

  • The first reports: "Terrible market. No one wears shoes, so there's no potential."
  • The second reports: "Wonderful market. No one wears shoes yet, so we can create the market and become the leader."

Identical data, opposite conclusions. Interpreting findings needs creativity and insight, not just statistics.

6Why marketing research fails

Issue Explanation
1. Narrow conception of research Managers treat it as fact-finding: "design a questionnaire, survey, report", without defining the problem or the decisions needed. When it's useless, they conclude research is useless
2. Uneven calibre of researchers Treated as a clerical activity, so it gets low pay and weak researchers, which brings poor results and reinforces the prejudice: a vicious circle
3. Poor framing of the problem New Coke: lots of taste research, but the real issue was how consumers felt about Coca-Cola as a brand, not taste in isolation
4. Late and occasionally wrong findings Managers want accurate, conclusive results tomorrow, but good research takes time and money
5. Personality and presentation differences Managers want concrete, simple, certain answers; reports seem abstract, complicated, tentative. Progressive firms put researchers in product teams
