## What is a business market?

The **business market** consists of **all organisations that acquire goods and services used to produce other products or services** that are sold, rented or supplied to others.

**Major industries in it:** agriculture, forestry and fisheries; mining; manufacturing; construction; transport; communication; public utilities; banking, finance and insurance; distribution; services.

:::example A simple pair of shoes
**Hide dealers** sell to **tanners**, who sell leather to **shoe manufacturers**, who sell to **wholesalers**, who sell to **retailers**, who sell to **consumers**. Each party also buys many other goods and services.

So **far more money and items change hands in B2B** than in consumer sales.
:::

## How business markets differ from consumer markets

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fewer, larger buyers** | Far fewer but **much bigger** customers | Goodyear depends on a few major car makers; aircraft engines, defence |
| **Close supplier–customer relationship** | Offers are **customised** to individual customers. Buyers often pick suppliers **who also buy from them** | A paper maker buys chemicals from a chemical firm that buys its paper |
| **Professional purchasing** | **Trained purchasing agents** follow policies; formal **requests for quotation, proposals, contracts** | Marketers must give **more technical data** |
| **Several buying influences** | **Buying committees** of technical experts and senior managers | Need trained **sales teams** |
| **Multiple sales calls** | It takes **4–4.5 calls** to close an average industrial sale (McGraw-Hill); capital projects can take **years** | — |
| **Derived demand** | B2B demand is **derived from consumer demand**, so watch consumers and the economy | Minivan popularity drove steel demand. In a recession, firms cut investment |
| **Inelastic demand** | Total demand **barely changes with price**, especially short-term, or when the item is a small part of total cost | Shoe makers won't buy much more leather if it gets cheaper; shoelaces |
| **Fluctuating demand** | **More volatile**. A small rise in consumer demand causes a large rise in demand for plant and equipment: the **acceleration effect** | +10% consumer demand → up to **+200%** business demand; −10% → collapse |
| **Geographically concentrated buyers** | Buyers cluster in regions, which **reduces selling costs** | Over half of US business buyers are in 7 states |
| **Direct purchasing** | Often **buy directly from manufacturers**, especially for complex or expensive items | Mainframes, aircraft |

## Buying situations

There are three, according to **Patrick Robinson** and others. The number of decisions depends on **problem complexity, newness, the number of people involved, and the time required**.

| Situation | Meaning | Example | Supplier implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Straight rebuy** | **Routine reorder** from an **approved list** of suppliers | Office supplies, bulk chemicals | **In-suppliers** maintain quality and offer **automatic reordering**. **Out-suppliers** offer something new or exploit dissatisfaction: win a small order, then grow it |
| **Modified rebuy** | The buyer wants to **change specs, prices, delivery or terms**. More participants on both sides | New supplier terms for packaging | In-suppliers get **nervous** and protect the account; out-suppliers see an **opportunity** |
| **New task** | Buying something **for the first time** | Office building, new security system | **The greater the cost or risk**, the more participants, the more information-gathering, and the **longer** the decision |

## Systems buying & selling

- **Systems buying:** many buyers prefer to buy **a total solution from one seller**.
- It originated in **government purchases** of weapons and communication systems. A **prime contractor** assembles the package from second-tier contractors, giving a **turnkey solution**: the buyer just "turns one key".
- **Systems selling** is the seller's version, used as a marketing tool.

:::example Systems contracting
A **single supplier provides the buyer's entire MRO** (maintenance, repair, operating) requirement and **manages the customer's inventory**. *Shell Oil* manages many customers' oil inventories and knows when to replenish.

- **Customer benefits:** lower procurement and management costs, **price protection**.
- **Seller benefits:** **steady demand**, lower operating costs, less paperwork.
:::

Systems selling is key when bidding for **large-scale industrial projects**: dams, steel factories, irrigation, sanitation, pipelines, utilities, even new towns. Firms compete on price, quality, reliability and other attributes.

## The buying centre — seven roles

**Webster and Wind:** the **buying centre** is the decision-making unit of a buying organisation. It is **all the individuals and groups who participate in the purchasing decision, share common goals and share the risks**.

| Role | Who / what |
|---|---|
| **1. Initiators** | **Request** that something be bought (users or others) |
| **2. Users** | **Will use** the product. Often initiate and help define requirements |
| **3. Influencers** | **Influence** the decision: define specs, provide evaluation information. **Technical staff** are key |
| **4. Deciders** | **Decide** on product requirements or suppliers |
| **5. Approvers** | **Authorise** the proposed actions of deciders or buyers |
| **6. Buyers** | Have **formal authority to select suppliers and negotiate terms**. Can include senior managers for complex buys |
| **7. Gatekeepers** | **Control the flow of sellers and information** into the buying centre: purchasing agents, receptionists, telephone operators |

:::tip Remember
- **Several people can hold one role**, and **one person can hold several roles**. A purchasing manager is often **buyer + influencer + gatekeeper** at once.
- A typical buying centre has **5–6 members, often dozens**, and can include **outsiders** (government officials, consultants, technical advisers).
- **Purchasing agents** dominate straight and modified rebuys and **supplier selection**. **Other departments** (e.g. engineering) dominate **new tasks** and **component selection**.

Memory hook: "**I U I D A B G**": **I**nitiators, **U**sers, **I**nfluencers, **D**eciders, **A**pprovers, **B**uyers, **G**atekeepers.
:::

## Procurement: orientations & Kraljic's four product types

Business buyers seek the **highest benefit package** (economic, technical, service, social) **relative to cost**. **Purchase incentive ∝ perceived benefits ÷ costs.** Purchasing has been **upgraded**, sometimes to vice-president level, so sellers must upgrade their salespeople too.

### Three purchasing orientations

| Orientation | Focus | Tactics |
|---|---|---|
| **Buying** | **Short-term, tactical**: lowest price for a given quality | **Commoditisation** (treat it as a commodity; care only about price) and **multisourcing** (make several suppliers compete) |
| **Procurement** | **Quality improvement and cost reduction** together | **Collaborative relationships**, early supplier involvement, JIT, **long-term contracts**, MRP |
| **Supply chain management** | **Strategic, value-adding** | A **seamless supply chain** from raw materials to on-time delivery to end users |

## The 8 buy phases (Robinson et al.)

:::flow
1. Problem recognition
2. General need description & product specification
3. Supplier search
4. E-procurement
5. Proposal solicitation
6. Supplier selection
7. Order-routine specification
8. Performance review
:::

| # | Stage | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Problem recognition** | **Internal** triggers: new product, machine breakdown, unsatisfactory material, a chance to get lower prices or better quality. **External** triggers: trade shows, ads, sales calls. Marketers stimulate it with **direct mail, telemarketing, calling on prospects** |
| 2 | **General need description & product specification** | Define the item's **characteristics and quantity**. For complex items, work with engineers and users on reliability, durability and price |
| 3 | **Supplier search** | **Trade directories, contacts, trade ads, trade shows, the Internet**. B2B e-commerce far outstrips B2C |
| 4 | **E-procurement** | Web **e-hubs**: **vertical hubs** (industries such as plastics, steel, chemicals, paper) and **functional hubs** (logistics, media buying, advertising, energy management) |
| 5 | **Proposal solicitation** | Invite qualified suppliers to submit **proposals**, then **formal presentations**. Proposals should be **marketing documents** describing value in customer terms |
| 6 | **Supplier selection** | Specify supplier attributes and weights; use **supplier-evaluation models** and **customer value assessment** (CVA: 8 methods). Attributes depend on the situation: **routine** (delivery reliability, price, reputation), **procedural-problem** such as a copier (technical service, flexibility, reliability), **political-problem** such as a computer system (price, reputation, product and service reliability, flexibility) |
| 7 | **Order-routine specification** | Final order: specs, quantity, delivery time, returns, warranties. **Leasing** heavy equipment (lessee conserves capital and gets the latest models). **Blanket contracts** (**stockless purchase plans**): the supplier resupplies as needed at agreed prices; the buyer's computer orders automatically, which locks suppliers in |
| 8 | **Performance review** | Ask **end users**, use **weighted-score ratings**, or **add up the cost of poor performance**. Then continue, modify or drop the supplier |

:::tip Consumer vs business decision process
Consumers go through **5 stages** (Week 5). Business buyers go through **8 buy phases**, with more formality, more people, written specs and proposals, and supplier evaluation.
:::

## Business-to-business relationships

- Closer relationships are driven by **supply chain management, early supplier involvement and purchasing alliances**.
- **Vertical coordination** goes beyond transactions to create **more value for both sides**. **Trust** is a prerequisite.

**What drove ad-agency–client relationships** (a historical study):

1. **Market growth** for one partner (mass production led to national brands and mass advertising).
2. **Information asymmetry**: partnering beats invading the other's area (agencies' specialist knowledge).
3. **High entry barriers** for one partner.
4. **Dependence asymmetry**: one partner controls the other (agencies controlled media access).
5. **Economies of scale** from the relationship.

**Cannon & Perreault** classified buyer–supplier relationships using **four factors**: **availability of alternatives, importance of supply, complexity of supply, supply market dynamism**. They found **eight relationship types**:

| Type | Character |
|---|---|
| **Basic buying and selling** | Simple, routine; moderate cooperation |
| **Bare bones** | More seller adaptation, less cooperation |
| **Contractual transaction** | Low trust; defined by a **formal contract** |
| **Customer supply** | Traditional custom supply; **competition** dominates |
| **Cooperative systems** | Operationally close, no structural commitment |
| **Collaborative** | **High trust and commitment**: true partnership |
| **Mutually adaptive** | Lots of relationship-specific adaptation, not necessarily trust |
| **Customer is king** | The seller adapts to the customer without expecting change in return |

:::key When are close relationships worth it?
**Closest relationships** arise when **supply is important** to the customer and there are **procurement obstacles**: complex requirements, few alternative suppliers. Some buyers just want basic performance, and some suppliers won't invest in low-growth customers.
:::
