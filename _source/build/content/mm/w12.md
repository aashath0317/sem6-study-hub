## Editing tells the intended story

:::key Video editing
Video editing selects, orders and combines shots, sound and other elements so that the audience receives the intended message. Removing a shaky shot is only one small part of editing. The editor also controls meaning, continuity, emphasis, pace and the presentation of the complete story.
:::

Start with the purpose and storyboard. Ask what each shot adds, why it appears at that moment and whether the audience can follow the sequence. A technically attractive clip can still be unsuitable if it communicates the wrong idea. Effects should support the story rather than compete with it.

:::example A local story
A short video about a Galle craft workshop can open with the location, show the craftsperson at work, move closer to the details, and finish with the completed object. Randomly joining beautiful shots may leave viewers unsure who made the object or how it was made. Editing turns separate recordings into an understandable sequence.
:::

The lesson's outcomes are practical: describe non-linear editing, create a video in editing software, apply appropriate effects, and compress the result for distribution. The workflow below expands its outline into a complete guided production example. No additional official quiz or past paper was supplied for this week.

## Linear and non-linear editing

Traditional film could be physically cut and joined. Tape-based linear video editing instead copied selected shots to a master tape in their final sequence. It usually required real-time copying. Inserting or deleting a scene could require re-recording the later material, making substantial changes slow.

**Non-linear editing**, or **NLE**, uses random access to digital media. The editor can visit and rearrange clips in any order. A project stores decisions such as source references, in/out points, positions, effects and track levels. It generally preserves the original source files. The final production is rendered or exported from those decisions.

| Feature | Linear tape editing | Non-linear digital editing |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Sequential | Random access |
| Assembly | Copy clips onto a master in order | Arrange source references on a timeline |
| Major insertion | Often re-copy later scenes | Insert or move clips and adjust the edit |
| Original footage | Separate source tapes | Source files remain available |
| Revisions | Time-consuming and restrictive | Flexible, with undo and alternate versions |
| Output | Recorded master | Exported delivery file |

“Non-destructive” means the project can change the edit without permanently rewriting the original media. It does not protect against deleting the source files. Keep the project and its linked assets together and maintain a backup. A small project file alone is usually not the finished movie.

The PDF lists Premiere, Final Cut and Media Composer as professional NLE examples. It also names Windows Live Movie Maker and iMovie as simpler alternatives. These are historical course examples; the statement that a particular product is bundled or free is not a current purchasing guide. Learn the editing concepts because menus and product availability change.

## Post-production workflow and workspace

:::flow A purposeful edit
Import and organise source media
Set a sequence for the intended output
Review takes and mark in/out points
Build a rough assembly from the storyboard
Refine timing, continuity and sound
Apply needed effects, titles and transitions
Export a delivery copy
Watch and check the exported file
:::

An editing workspace normally includes a **project/media panel** for organised assets, a **source monitor** for reviewing a clip, a **timeline** for sequence order and tracks, a **programme monitor** for the current edit, an **effects panel**, and **audio meters**. Their arrangement varies between software packages.

Before cutting, choose consistent sequence settings: frame dimensions, frame rate, aspect ratio and audio settings. If footage differs, decide how the software should scale or interpret it. Avoid accidentally stretching a picture. Keep the original recordings and use organised folders or bins for interviews, supporting pictures, music, graphics and exports.

**In point** marks where a selected part starts. **Out point** marks where it ends. Trimming changes these boundaries. Splitting creates separate timeline pieces. Moving changes order or position. A gap can produce black video or silence. An overlap may be used for a transition, but does not automatically make the story clearer.

In a ripple edit, changing a clip's duration also shifts later material to close or create space. When only one track moves, speech or music may lose synchronisation. Check linked picture and sound, track locks and downstream changes after each substantial edit.

## Shots, A-Roll and B-Roll

A **wide shot** establishes a location and spatial relationships. A **medium shot** shows a person and some surrounding action. A **close-up** directs attention to a face, hand or object detail. An **insert** can make a small but important action readable. Choose coverage so that the editor has enough appropriate views to explain the scene.

The linked [Pond5 explanation](https://blog.pond5.com/8324-a-roll-and-b-roll-the-two-types-of-footage-you-need-to-tell-a-great-story/) distinguishes **A-Roll**, the main narrative footage, from **B-Roll**, supporting views. In an interview-led piece, the speaker carries the explanation while relevant detail shots show what is being discussed. A cutaway can also hide a necessary edit while the main narration continues.

For the workshop example, keep the craftsperson's explanation as the narrative. Show hands using a tool when that tool is mentioned. Return to the person when their expression matters. Supporting footage must match the claim and context; an unrelated attractive picture can mislead.

Continuity means that the audience can understand the action and its relationships across edits. Watch movement direction, object positions, eyelines and changes in light or sound. A shot that works alone may not connect cleanly with its neighbours. Review the whole sequence with sound, then check whether its visual story remains understandable when muted.

## Cuts and transitions

The linked [PremiumBeat cuts guide](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/8-essential-cuts-every-editor-should-know/) covers these eight groups. The compact descriptions below are in our own words.

| Cut | What happens |
|---|---|
| Standard/hard cut | One shot immediately replaces another |
| Jump cut | A visible time jump occurs within related material |
| L cut | Earlier audio continues beneath the next picture |
| J cut | Next audio starts before its picture |
| Cutting on action | An action continues across the edit |
| Cutaway | A related detail interrupts the main view |
| Cross-cut | The edit alternates between separate actions |
| Montage | Selected short views condense a process or passage of time |
| Match cut | Similar visual movement or form connects shots |

The article groups L and J cuts together, so the table has separate rows for the paired concepts. Do not count them as a claim that the article lists a different number of groups.

A **transition** controls how the boundary between clips is presented. A straight cut is often sufficient. A dissolve briefly blends images and can suggest a change of time or place. A fade moves between a picture and a plain field. A wipe replaces one region with another. Use a transition because of its meaning, not because it exists in the effects library.

## Effects, titles and audio finishing

An effects library supplies reusable operations or presets. Colour correction can make neighbouring shots more consistent. Cropping or reframing can focus attention. Stabilisation can reduce some camera movement, though it may crop the frame. Keyframed properties change over time. Preview the result at the intended size and inspect the exported version; a preview may use reduced quality.

Titles provide a name, speaker role, location, section label or closing instruction. Keep wording concise, use sufficient contrast and allow enough reading time. A small decorative font can become unreadable on a phone. Place important text away from edges and check it against light and dark backgrounds. Captions carry spoken content and useful sound information; a decorative title is not a substitute for captions.

Balance speech, music and effects deliberately. Background music should not mask the explanation. Check abrupt cuts, changes in room tone, clipping and excessive noise. Use short fades where they prevent clicks or smooth an entry, and preserve intentional pauses. Headphones reveal some problems, while ordinary speakers reveal others. Check both where available.

## Compression and delivery checks

Export creates a playable file; saving the editing project preserves the editable decisions. Select a container and compatible codecs, target dimensions, frame rate, bit rate and audio settings for the receiving system. Higher delivery bit rate can preserve detail but increases file size. Lower dimensions may be suitable for a small display, though they remove spatial detail.

Maintain a good-quality master and create a separate delivery copy. Avoid repeatedly exporting a lossy copy and then editing that copy, because repeated lossy encoding can accumulate damage. Check the delivered file from beginning to end for missing media, incorrect aspect ratio, black gaps, unreadable titles, sync errors, clipping and an abrupt ending.

A local file, an intranet player and a public web service may accept different settings. Distribution constraints include network capacity, supported formats, file-size limits, audience device capability and the required duration. Test the actual exported file through the intended route, rather than assuming a successful timeline preview proves successful distribution.

## Activity 12.1: complete model production

**Question:** prepare a music video using existing music and video clips gathered from several sources.

**Model answer:** choose a coherent theme, such as a student's journey to a community arts event. Use music and pictures for which reuse is permitted, and record the required credits. Collect views of preparing materials, travelling, arriving, setting up and the finished event. Organise these into bins, noting their source, technical settings and allowed uses.

Listen to the track and mark its opening, main phrases and ending. Prepare a storyboard that follows the theme. Create a sequence with consistent dimensions and frame rate. Import the music first if its structure drives the edit, then arrange a rough picture sequence. Trim shots to remove accidental starts and stops. Use wide views to orient the audience and close-ups to show important details. Match significant actions or changes of scene to appropriate musical moments without cutting so rapidly that the story disappears.

Refine continuity and timing. Use a restrained transition at a meaningful change, rather than placing a different effect on every edit. Add an opening title and a closing credit. Correct obvious differences in exposure and colour. Balance any included live sound beneath or around the music, preserving clarity. Watch the full timeline and ask whether an unfamiliar viewer understands the theme.

Export a high-quality master and a compatible compressed copy. Watch the copy on a phone and a larger screen, checking shape, captions or titles, sync and the complete ending. Keep the project, media inventory and source permissions so the result can be revised. This is a complete model workflow for the practical activity; it does not claim that a separate music-video file has been filmed or submitted.
