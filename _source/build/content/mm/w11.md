## Video organisation and digitization

:::key Digital video
A video is a time-ordered sequence of images called **frames**. The picture is two-dimensional; time adds another dimension. Digital video stores numerical samples rather than a continuously varying voltage or magnetic pattern.
:::

A camera turns a view of the world into an image sequence. **Sampling** divides time into frames and each frame into spatial samples called pixels. **Quantization** assigns a discrete value to each sampled amplitude or colour component. Temporal sampling determines frame rate. Spatial sampling determines frame resolution. Quantization determines the possible colour values.

The lesson calls frame rate, frame resolution and colour depth the three resolutions of video. They affect both perceived quality and uncompressed capacity. Reducing them can reduce size, but can also remove motion detail, spatial detail or colour precision. Choose values for the actual application and audience.

:::flow Redrawing of Figure 11.4
Scene → camera signal
Sampling in time → successive frames
Sampling in space → pixels in each frame
Quantization → numerical colour values
Encoding and storage → a playable digital stream
:::

Analogue recording can vary continuously, but the recording medium introduces imperfections and noise. Digital copying can preserve the numerical data when transmission and decoding are correct. Neither “digital always contains more information” nor “analogue is always better” is a general rule. Quality depends on capture, sampling, processing and delivery.

## Component, separated and composite signals

| Signal | Organisation | Main benefit | Main cost or limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Component | Three separate component paths; RGB, YUV or YIQ are possible | Avoids mixing the separate components | More connections, bandwidth and synchronisation |
| S-Video | Separate luminance and chrominance paths | Avoids luminance/chrominance crosstalk | Still carries colour components together |
| Composite | Luminance and chrominance mixed into one signal | Simple single video connection | Receiver must separate them; interference can remain |

**Luminance** describes brightness information. **Chrominance** describes colour information. The PDF's connector photographs show three plugs for component, a multi-pin S-Video connector and one composite plug. The number of signal paths does not mean the same number of complete video pictures.

In component systems the paths need good synchronisation. S-Video preserves separate brightness because visual detail depends strongly on it. Composite places chrominance in a higher-frequency part of the shared channel; imperfect separation can cause visible artifacts. These are signal arrangements, not video file extensions.

## Progressive and interlaced scanning

**Progressive scanning** draws all the lines of a frame in sequence. **Interlaced scanning** draws alternate line sets in separate fields: one field carries odd lines and the next even lines. There are two fields per interlaced frame. Interlacing historically reduced visible flicker under transmission constraints. Fields captured at different times can show comb-like edges on motion when combined without suitable processing.

| Notation in the lesson | Meaning and context |
|---|---|
| 525/59.94/2:1 | Total lines, field rate, interlaced; North American/Japanese historical family |
| 625/50/2:1 | Total lines, field rate, interlaced; European/Asian historical family |
| 1125/60/2:1 | Historical high-definition interlaced family |
| 1920×1080, 1080i | Full-HD spatial dimensions with interlaced presentation |
| 1280×720, 720p | HD spatial dimensions with progressive presentation |

Do not confuse fields per second with frames per second. Total scanning lines include blanking and synchronisation overhead. Active picture lines are fewer. The PDF describes historical conventional pictures as 644×483 and mentions an earlier 1920×1035 HDTV system with non-square pixels. These are source-specific historical descriptions, not universal dimensions for every standard-definition or HD file.

The source gives basic component sampling rates of **13.5 MHz** for the standard-definition families and **74.25 MHz** for the historical high-definition family. Its analogue studio bandwidth examples are about **5.5 MHz**, **6.5 MHz** and **30 MHz**, respectively. Sampling frequency and analogue channel bandwidth are different quantities.

## Standards, dimensions and colour

| Historical colour standard | Scan family | Frame rate | Colour model | Source bandwidth details |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NTSC | 525 lines, interlaced | 29.97 fps | YIQ | Channel about 6.0 MHz; Y 4.2, I 1.2, Q 0.6 MHz |
| PAL | 625 lines, interlaced | 25 fps | YUV | Channel about 8 MHz; Y 5.5, U 1.8, V 1.8 MHz |
| SECAM | 625 lines, interlaced | 25 fps | YUV-related colour representation | Channel about 8 MHz; source lists Y 6.0, U 2.0, V 2.0 MHz |

The source associates NTSC with North America and Japan, PAL with Germany, the United Kingdom, India and China, and SECAM with France and Eastern Europe. It describes NTSC blanking as 20 lines at the beginning of each field, leaving 485 active picture lines in its simplified account. NTSC's 29.97 fps is sometimes rounded to 30 fps in the lesson.

PAL alternates chroma phase across successive lines. SECAM sends colour-difference information on alternate lines using frequency modulation. The lesson contrasts PAL's greater spatial line count with NTSC's greater temporal frame rate. Do not add the listed component bandwidths to obtain channel bandwidth: component spectra and their transmission arrangements differ. The PDF presents simplified historical values.

:::

## Raw video data size: complete workings

:::formula
Video bytes = width × height × frames/second × (bits/pixel ÷ 8) × seconds
Audio bytes = channels × samples/second × (bits/sample ÷ 8) × seconds
Total bytes = video bytes + audio bytes
Decimal MB = bytes ÷ 1,000,000
:::

These estimates exclude headers, metadata, compression and transmission overhead. Convert minutes to seconds and bits to bytes before multiplying. This lesson uses decimal MB, matching its example.

:::example Solved size example A: the PDF example, corrected
Given: **320×240**, **30 fps**, **8-bit** colour, **60 s**; audio **44,100 Hz**, **8-bit**, mono.

Video formula → **320 × 240 × 30 × (8/8) × 60**.
Working → **76,800 × 30 × 1 × 60 = 138,240,000 bytes = 138.24 MB**.
Audio formula → **1 × 44,100 × (8/8) × 60 = 2,646,000 bytes = 2.646 MB**.
Answer → **140,886,000 bytes = 140.886 MB**, approximately **140.89 MB**.

The PDF writes “44100 kHz” in the substitution; it means **44,100 Hz**. It rounds audio to **2.6 MB** before addition and obtains **140.84 MB**. Keep full precision until the final answer.
:::

:::example Solved size example B: video only
Given **640×480**, **25 fps**, **24 bits/pixel**, **10 s**.
Formula → W × H × r × (b/8) × t.
Substitution → **640 × 480 × 25 × (24/8) × 10**.
Working → **307,200 × 25 × 3 × 10 = 230,400,000**.
Answer → **230,400,000 bytes = 230.4 MB**, video only.
:::

:::example Solved size example C: video plus stereo sound
Given **1280×720**, **25 fps**, **24 bits/pixel**, **4 s**; **48,000 Hz**, **16-bit**, stereo sound.
Video → **1280 × 720 × 25 × 3 × 4 = 276,480,000 bytes**.
Audio → **2 × 48,000 × 2 × 4 = 768,000 bytes**.
Total → **276,480,000 + 768,000 = 277,248,000 bytes = 277.248 MB**.
:::

## Bit rate and compression ratio

:::formula
Raw video bit rate = width × height × fps × bits/pixel
Average bit rate = file bytes × 8 ÷ duration in seconds
Compression ratio = uncompressed size ÷ compressed size
Compressed size = uncompressed size ÷ compression ratio
:::

A ratio **R:1** means the uncompressed quantity is **R** times the compressed quantity. Compare the same units. **Mb/s** means megabits per second; **MB/s** means megabytes per second. Real network provisioning also needs overhead and capacity for variable-rate peaks.

:::example Three solved bit-rate examples
**Activity 11.1(a):** **320×240**, **12 fps**, **8 bits/pixel**. Formula → W × H × r × b. Substitution → **320 × 240 × 12 × 8**. Working → **76,800 × 96 = 7,372,800**. Answer → minimum raw video payload **7,372,800 bit/s = 7.3728 Mb/s**, excluding audio and overhead.

**Example B:** **640×480**, **25 fps**, **24 bits/pixel**. Formula → W × H × r × b. Working → **640 × 480 × 25 × 24 = 184,320,000 bit/s = 184.32 Mb/s**.

**Example C:** a **15,000,000-byte** delivered file lasts **30 s**. Formula → bytes × 8 ÷ seconds. Working → **15,000,000 × 8 ÷ 30 = 4,000,000 bit/s = 4 Mb/s**, average total file rate.
:::

:::example Three solved compression examples
**Example A:** raw **120 MB**, encoded **12 MB**. Ratio → **120 ÷ 12 = 10**, so **10:1**. Space saved → **120 − 12 = 108 MB**, or **90%**.

**Example B:** raw **300 MB**, ratio **25:1**. Compressed size → **300 ÷ 25 = 12 MB**.

**Example C:** encoded **8 MB**, ratio **15:1**. Raw size → **8 × 15 = 120 MB**. Ratio check → **120 ÷ 8 = 15:1**.
:::

## Codecs, containers and distribution

A **codec** encodes and decodes media. A **container** holds streams and metadata. A **player** understands the container and supports its codecs. The extension does not guarantee that every device can decode the content. Examples listed in the PDF include **FLV, MP4, MOV, WMV, WebM, RealMedia, AVI** and **MPG**. AVI and MOV can hold different codecs; they are not themselves one compression algorithm.

**Lossless compression** preserves the original numerical data exactly after decoding. **Lossy compression** discards information to reduce capacity and rate, accepting some distortion. Video compression exploits similarities within pictures and between nearby frames. High compression can introduce artifacts and decoding demands. Keep a good-quality master and make delivery copies for the target.

**Activity 11.1(b), model answer:** MPEG-1 is associated with VCD-era lower-resolution delivery; it has broad historical support but limited quality efficiency. MPEG-2 is associated with DVD and broadcast workflows; it is established but generally needs more data than later efficient codecs for comparable quality. MPEG-4 is a family of standards, not one extension or one algorithm; particular codecs such as MPEG-4 AVC/H.264 offer efficient distribution but require encoding/decoding work and compatibility checks. RLE is lossless and useful on repeated runs, but poorly suited to complex noisy footage. Cinepak, Indeo and Microsoft's original Video codec are historical AVI examples. Choose according to quality, bandwidth, device support and editing needs. The source's “current popular” wording and claims that MOV always needs QuickTime installation are historical, not universal current rules.

## Production process and Activity 11.3

**Pre-production** sets the purpose, audience, message, script, storyboard, shot list, locations, permissions, equipment, crew, schedule and budget. **Production** captures planned picture and sound, checks exposure and focus, monitors audio, records useful extra shots and backs up material. **Post-production** assembles the story, trims shots, adds suitable titles and effects, adjusts colour and sound, then exports and checks delivery copies.

The linked [Venture Videos production guide](https://www.venturevideos.com/insight/corporate-video-production-guide) emphasises measurable objectives, audience research, a focused message and a planned distribution strategy before filming. Those planning decisions guide the script, production resources and how success will be measured. The linked [eight-step video](https://youtu.be/OdZPuE0mHs8) could not be fetched during preparation; no unseen video content is claimed here.

**Activity 11.3 question:** prepare a corporate-video pre-production plan for a selected topic.

**Model plan: a Sri Lankan tea cooperative's visitor introduction.** Purpose: explain the cooperative's production and invite visitors to book a tour. Audience: prospective local and overseas visitors. Message: see how growers turn harvested leaves into a finished product. Deliverable: a short web video with readable captions and a clear booking action. Script: opening plantation view, grower introduction, processing explanation, visitor experience and booking instruction. Storyboard each shot, narration and title. Shot list includes location views, an interview, leaf handling and processing details. Obtain location and contributor permission, plan filming around weather and machinery noise, assign producer, camera operator and sound responsibilities, reserve equipment, and schedule editing and review. Estimate travel, crew and post-production costs. Prepare an alternative indoor shoot plan, record clean narration, and check the final file on the intended phone and website before publication.
