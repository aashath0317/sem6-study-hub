1Why price matters

Price is the only revenue earner

In the marketing mix (4 Ps: product, price, place, promotion), price is the only element that brings in revenue. All the others are costs. Price also communicates the value positioning of the product 01:01.

A firm must set a price for the first time when 02:29:

  1. it develops a new product
  2. it introduces its regular product into a new distribution channel or geographical area (e.g. a Sri Lankan company entering India)
Price = Cost + Profit

2The pricing policy: six factors

  1. Select the pricing objective
  1. Determine demand
  1. Estimate costs
  1. Analyse competitors (costs, prices, offers)
  1. Select a pricing method
  1. Select the final price

1. Pricing objectives 04:44

Objective Typical price
Survival Low, enough to cover costs and stay in the market
Maximise current profit Price that gives the best profit now
Maximise market share Low, to build volume
Maximise market skimming High at launch, falling later
Product-quality leadership High, to signal premium quality

2. Demand & price elasticity

  • Demand and price are inversely related: a higher price means lower demand, and vice versa (the chocolate example: buy 2, then 5 when the price falls) 06:57.
  • Price elasticity measures how demand changes with a unit change in price 08:03.
Change in demand when price changes
Price inelastic Little or no change
Price elastic Significant change

Demand is likely to be less elastic when:

  • there are few or no substitutes (rice in Sri Lanka 08:40)
  • buyers are slow to change their buying habits
  • buyers think the higher prices are justified

3. Estimating costs 10:39

  • Fixed costs don't change with output: the same whether you make 1 or 100.
  • Variable costs change with output: zero output means zero variable cost.
  • Target costing works backwards: market price − required profit = cost you must achieve.

3Markup vs margin

The difference 12:50
  • Markup: profit as a % of cost
  • Margin: profit as a % of selling price
25% each way 13:36
Cost markup 25% Profit margin 25%
Cost 100 75 (target cost)
Profit 25 (25% of cost) 25 (25% of price)
Selling price 125 100
Best for New, introductory products (cost known, set the price) Established products (market price known, target the cost)
Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup) Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin)
e.g. 25% markup = 20% margin; 25% margin = 33.3% markup

4Pricing methods

Cost-plus pricing

Set the price at the production cost (goods plus fixed costs at the current volume) plus a profit margin or markup 15:10.

Full-cost plus Marginal-cost plus
Cost base Variable + fixed costs Variable costs only
Markup Smaller (covers profit only) Larger (must also cover fixed costs)
Product A, 20% markup 16:02

Direct material 60, direct labour 30, variable overhead 90, fixed overhead 20.

Full cost Marginal cost
Direct material 60 60
Direct labour 30 30
Variable overhead 90 90
Fixed overhead 20 –

Correction

The video says the marginal-cost price is 260 17:28. That's a slip: 180 × 1.20 = 216.

Limitation of cost-plus

It ignores demand. It also ignores competitors and what customers are willing to pay.

Other methods

Method Idea Example
Target return pricing Set the price to achieve a target return on investment (ROI) 18:11 "We need Rs 1 million profit. What price gets us there?"
Value-based pricing Price on the value it creates for the customer 18:40 Plain ice cream Rs 100; decorated sundae Rs 200
Psychological pricing Lessen the impact of the price in the customer's mind, or signal quality or esteem 19:20 29.99 feels "less than 30"
Geographical pricing Different prices at different locations; may involve barter, countertrade, foreign currency 20:29
Skimming pricing High introductory price with heavy promotion, slowly dropped later 01:34 Unique products not easily copied; recovers development cost
Penetration pricing Low initial price to reach the largest share of the target market 02:04 Price-sensitive consumers; discourages new competitors
Status quo pricing Match or stay close to competitors' prices 03:38 Clothing companies, airlines
Choco Brocco 00:07

Farmer Joe's daughters spent $10,000 developing a chocolate-tasting broccoli.

  • Skimming fits: the product is unique, they want to recoup the investment, and there's no competitor.
  • Penetration risks:
  • shortages if demand outruns supply
  • diluting the brand (the luxury-car starter-sedan example 03:09)
  • Status quo is unnecessary: nobody else sells it.

Decision: $5 a bunch vs $3 for ordinary broccoli, a skimming price 04:47.

Discounts & allowances

  • early payment and cash discounts
  • off-season discounts
  • bulk purchase (quantity) discounts
  • retail discount
  • trade allowance

Discriminatory pricing

The same product at different prices by:

  • customer segment (student or senior rates)
  • product form (versions or packaging)
  • location (theatre seats)
  • time (peak vs off-peak)

Q2: Cost and price of a plastic chair (Amaranath Enterprises)

1. Redistribute the service departments (simultaneous equations):

  • A (packing) = 6,000 + 0.25B
  • B (maintenance) = 5,000 + 0.2A
  • So A = 7,631.58 and B = 6,526.32

Chair plant: 25,000 + 0.8 × 7,631.58 + 0.75 × 6,526.32 = 25,000 + 6,105.26 + 4,894.74 = 36,000

2. Absorption rate: 300 chairs × 5 hours = 1,500 direct labour hours, so 36,000 / 1,500 = Rs. 24 per hour
