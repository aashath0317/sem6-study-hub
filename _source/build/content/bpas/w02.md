## What is accounting?

:::key Definitions
- **American Accounting Association (1974):** "Accounting is the process of **identifying, measuring and communicating** information to permit **informed judgements and decisions** by users of the information."
- **Course definition:** accounting is the process of **collecting and recording** business transactions and events, **summarising** them, and **communicating the results** to interested parties to support **economic decision-making**.
:::

:::flow
Collecting
Recording
Classifying
Summarising
Analysing
Interpreting
Communicating to stakeholders
:::

### The accounting entity concept
**The business is separate from its owner.** The business's accounts record only the **business's** transactions. The owner's personal spending isn't business expense, and the owner's investment is **capital** (a claim of the owner on the business).

### Objectives of accounting
1. **Control and protect** the business's resources.
2. Find the **operating profit or loss** and the **financial position**.
3. Support **rational decision-making**.
4. Provide the **required information to stakeholders**.

## Stakeholders and what they need

A **stakeholder** is a person or entity with an **interest in the economic performance** of the business.

| Stakeholder | Needs information to… |
|---|---|
| **(a) Investors** (providers of risk capital) | Assess **risk and return**; decide whether to **buy, hold or sell**; assess the ability to pay **dividends** |
| **(b) Employees** | Assess **stability and profitability**; the ability to provide **pay, retirement benefits, jobs** |
| **(c) Lenders** | Decide whether **loans and interest will be repaid when due** |
| **(d) Suppliers and trade creditors** | Decide whether **amounts owed will be paid when due** (a **shorter-term** interest than lenders, unless the entity is a major customer) |
| **(e) Customers** | Assess the **continuance** of the entity, especially with long-term involvement or dependence |
| **(f) Government and agencies** | **Regulate**, set **taxation** policy, produce **national income** statistics |
| **(g) Public** | Contribution to the **local economy**: jobs, use of local suppliers |

## The Conceptual Framework for financial reporting

The framework covers:

- the **objective** of financial reporting
- the **qualitative characteristics** of useful information
- the **definition, recognition and measurement** of the elements
- the concepts of **capital and capital maintenance**

### Objective of general-purpose financial reporting

- **Primary users:** **present and potential investors, lenders and other creditors**, deciding whether to buy, sell or hold equity or debt and whether to provide or settle loans.
- **What they need:**
  - information to assess **future net cash inflows**
  - **stewardship**: how efficiently management used the resources
- Reports **can't give users everything**; they also use other sources.
- **Regulators are not primary users.**

| Information about… | Reported in |
|---|---|
| **Economic resources and claims** (strengths, liquidity, solvency, need for financing) | **Statement of financial position** |
| **Changes from financial performance** (accrual accounting) | **Statement of comprehensive income** |
| **Past cash flows** (how cash is obtained and spent: borrowing, repayments, dividends) | **Statement of cash flows** |
| **Changes not from performance** (issuing equity, distributions to owners) | **Statement of changes in equity** |

### Qualitative characteristics

:::grid
**Fundamental**

- **Relevance:** capable of **making a difference** to decisions, through **predictive and/or confirmatory value**. **Materiality** is an entity-specific aspect of relevance, based on the **nature or size** of an item.
- **Faithful representation:** **complete, neutral, free from error**.

Information must be **both** relevant and faithfully represented to be useful.
+++
**Enhancing**

- **Comparability:** across entities and across periods
- **Verifiability:** independent, knowledgeable observers could reach **consensus**
- **Timeliness:** available **in time** to influence decisions
- **Understandability:** classified, characterised and presented **clearly and concisely**, for users with **reasonable knowledge** who study it **diligently**

Enhancing characteristics **can't rescue** information that is irrelevant or unfaithful.
:::

- **Cost constraint:** reporting costs must be **justified by the benefits**.
- **Underlying assumption, going concern:** the entity is assumed to **continue operating indefinitely**. If not, it needs disclosure and a different basis.

### The five elements

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Asset** | A **resource controlled** by the entity as a result of **past events**, from which **future economic benefits** are expected to flow to it |
| **Liability** | A **present obligation** arising from **past events**, whose settlement is expected to cause an **outflow** of resources |
| **Equity** | The **residual interest** in assets after deducting all liabilities: **Equity = Assets − Liabilities** |
| **Income** | **Increases in economic benefits** (inflows or enhancements of assets, or decreases of liabilities) that **increase equity**, other than **owner contributions**. Includes **revenue** (sales, fees, interest, dividends, royalties, rent) and **gains** |
| **Expense** | **Decreases in economic benefits** (outflows or depletions of assets, or new liabilities) that **decrease equity**, other than **distributions to owners**. Includes ordinary expenses (cost of sales, wages, depreciation) and **losses** |

- **Financial position elements:** assets, liabilities, equity.
- **Performance elements:** income and expenses.
- **Profit = income − expenses.**

### Recognition & measurement
**Recognise** an item when:

1. it meets the **definition** of an element, **and**
2. future economic benefits flowing to or from the entity are **probable**, **and**
3. its cost or value can be **measured reliably**.

**Measurement bases:** **historical cost** (the most common), **current cost**, **net realisable (settlement) value**, **present value**.

## Debit & credit rules and the accounting equation

**Double entry (Luca Pacioli):** each party **receives something and gives something**. What is **received is a debit**; what is **given is a credit**.

| Account type | **Increase** | **Decrease** |
|---|---|---|
| **Assets** | **Debit** | Credit |
| **Expenses** | **Debit** | Credit |
| **Liabilities** | Credit | Debit |
| **Income** | Credit | Debit |
| **Capital** | Credit | Debit |

:::tip Memory hook: "DEAD CLIC"
**D**ebit increases **E**xpenses, **A**ssets, **D**rawings · **C**redit increases **L**iabilities, **I**ncome, **C**apital.
:::

:::formula
Assets = Capital
Assets = Capital + Liabilities
Assets = Capital + Profit + Liabilities
Assets = Capital + (Income − Expenses) + Liabilities
**Assets + Expenses = Capital + Income + Liabilities**  ← debit side = credit side
:::

## The T-account & balancing

- A **T-account** has a **title**.
- The **left side is the debit (Dr)**: what is **received**.
- The **right side is the credit (Cr)**: what is **given**.

**How to balance an account:**

1. **Foot** (total) the **larger** side.
2. Put that total on **both** sides.
3. The **difference** is the **balance carried down (c/d)** on the smaller side, then **brought down (b/d)** on the opposite side.

:::example Balancing the cash account
| Dr (Cash) | Rs | Cr (Cash) | Rs |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| | 3,750 | | 850 |
| | 4,300 | | 1,400 |
| | 2,900 | | 700 |
| | | | 2,900 |
| | | **Balance c/d** | **5,100** |
| **Total** | **10,950** | **Total** | **10,950** |
| **Balance b/d** | **5,100** | | |

- Debit side = 3,750 + 4,300 + 2,900 = **10,950**
- Credit side = 850 + 1,400 + 700 + 2,900 = 5,850
- **Balance = 10,950 − 5,850 = 5,100 Dr**: the cash on hand
:::

## Source documents & primary books

:::flow

:::

| Source document | Prepared by → sent to | Recorded in |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales invoice** | Business → customer (credit sale) | **Sales journal** |
| **Purchase invoice** | Supplier → business (credit purchase) | **Purchase journal** |
| **Debit note** | **Business (buyer) → supplier** when returning goods | **Purchase return journal** |
| **Credit note** | **Business (seller) → customer** when accepting returned goods | **Sales return journal** |
| **Receipt** and **payment voucher (PV)** | For cash received or paid | **Cash book** |
| **Journal voucher** | Internal, for other entries | **General journal** |

:::warn Careful: who issues the credit note?
The PDF says the credit note is "prepared by the customer". In practice, the **seller (the business) issues the credit note** to the customer to confirm it will reduce what the customer owes. The **buyer issues a debit note** to the supplier when returning goods. The double entries below are correct either way.
:::

## The four day books (primary entry journals)

| Journal | Records | Not recorded here | Source document | Double entry (from the totals) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Purchase journal** | **Credit purchases of goods** for resale | Cash purchases; **credit purchases of fixed assets** | **Purchase invoice** | **Dr Purchases / Cr Creditors** |
| **Purchase return journal** (return outwards) | Goods **returned to suppliers** | — | **Debit note** | **Dr Creditors / Cr Purchase returns** |
| **Sales journal** | **Credit sales of goods** | Cash sales; credit sales of fixed assets | **Sales invoice** | **Dr Debtors / Cr Sales** |
| **Sales return journal** (return inwards) | Goods **returned by customers** | — | **Credit note** | **Dr Sales returns / Cr Debtors** |

**Reasons goods are returned:**

- defective or faulty production
- wrong specification or type selected
- poor quality
- damaged goods
- goods other than those ordered

**Format of each journal:**

| Date | Invoice / note no. | Supplier / customer | Description | Qty | Unit price | Value | **L/F** | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**L/F** (ledger folio) is the reference to the ledger page where the entry is posted.
