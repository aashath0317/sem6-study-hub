1Product strategy

Product strategy is a high-level plan describing what a business hopes to achieve with its product and how. It answers:

  • Who will it serve (target market)?
  • How will it benefit them?
  • What are the product's goals over its life cycle?

It's the roadmap for developing a product. It covers quality, NPD, packaging, branding and R&D, and serves as the baseline for measuring success before, during and after production.

What is a product? (Kotler)

"Anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use or consumption that might satisfy a want or need." This includes physical goods, services (insurance, banking, movies), experiences, events, persons, places, properties, organisations, information and ideas.

2Three levels of a product

🧪 Try it — Product levels explorer
Level Meaning
Core product The fundamental benefit the customer really buys
Actual product What customers see physically: design, quality level, brand name, features, packaging
Augmented product Additional services and benefits that differentiate it from competitors: warranty, delivery, installation, after-sales service
Problems with augmentation
  1. Each augmentation adds cost.
  2. Augmented benefits soon become expected benefits, because competitors copy them.
  3. Competitors can offer the basic benefits at a lower cost (a stripped-down product).

3Product classifications

By durability and tangibility

Type Meaning Marketing implication
Non-durable goods Tangible, used up in one or a few uses, bought often (shampoo) Make available in many locations; advertise to induce trial and build preference
Durable goods Tangible, last longer, replaced less often (refrigerator) More personal selling and service, seller guarantees, higher margins
Services Activities, benefits or satisfactions offered for sale Manage the 4 characteristics below

The four characteristics of services:

  • Intangible: can't be seen, tasted, felt, heard or smelled before purchase
  • Inseparable: produced and consumed at the same time
  • Variable: quality depends on who, when and where
  • Perishable: can't be stored

Consumer goods, by shopping habits

Type Meaning Examples
Convenience goods Bought frequently, immediately, with minimum effort Soap, toothpaste, newspapers
↳ Staple Bought regularly Soap
↳ Impulse Bought without planning; displayed widely Candy bars at the checkout
↳ Emergency Bought because of an urgent need Umbrella in a rainstorm
Shopping goods Customers compare suitability, quality, price and style Clothes, furniture
Specialty goods Unique characteristics or brand identity: buyers make a special purchasing effort Computers (in the notes), luxury brands
Unsought goods Unknown to the consumer, or known but not normally thought of New products; life insurance

4Product mix and product line decisions

Product mix (assortment): all the product lines and items a seller offers.

Dimension Meaning
Width The number of different product lines
Length The total number of items across all lines
Depth The number of variants of each product
Consistency How closely related the lines are: end use, production, channels
🧪 Try it — Product mix calculator

Each line on its own row: Line name: item(variants), item(variants)

Four ways to grow using these dimensions:

  1. Widen: add new lines to serve more customers.
  2. Lengthen: add more items to each line.
  3. Deepen: add more variants.
  4. Pursue more or less consistency: a strong reputation in one field, or a presence in several.

Product line decisions

Kotler: a product line is a group of products that are closely related because they perform a similar function, are sold to the same customer groups, go through the same channels, or fall in a particular price range.

Decision Meaning
Product-line analysis Know each item's sales and profit and its position against competitors to decide which to build, maintain, harvest or divest
Line length Depends on production and marketing skills, finance and objectives. Firms with short lines can profit by adding items; mature firms with long lines can profit by dropping slow sellers
Line stretching Lengthen the line beyond its current range: downward, upward or both ways
Line filling Add items within the present range. Each must be noticeably different and meet a need
Line modernisation Periodically update styles, sizes and shapes (continuously in fast-changing markets)
Line pruning Cut poorly performing items

5Brands, brand meaning and brand equity

Definition (AMA)

A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol or design, or a combination of them, intended to identify the goods or services of one seller and differentiate them from competitors.

  • A brand name is the word or letters part.
  • A trademark is a legal term: the marks the law protects.
  • Brands never expire, unlike patents and copyrights.
  • Brands create expectations about purpose, performance, quality and price, which lets marketers add value and differentiate.
  • A brand is the seller's commitment to deliver consistent features, benefits and services. The best brands convey a warranty of quality.

Six levels of brand meaning (the Mercedes example)

Level Mercedes
Attributes Expensive, well-built, well-engineered, durable, prestigious
Benefits "Durable" means long use (functional); "expensive" means feeling important and admired (emotional)
Values High performance, safety, prestige
Culture German culture: organised, efficient, high quality
Personality A no-nonsense, powerful personality
User A 55-year-old top executive
Don't build a brand on attributes alone
  • Buyers care more about benefits.
  • Attributes are easily copied.
  • They may become less desirable later.

Don't rely on a single benefit either: competitors can out-promise you. Stay free to move to new benefit positions. The strongest brands work at the deepest levels: values, culture, personality.

Brand equity

Brands differ in power and value. They move from awareness → acceptability → preference → loyalty.

Aaker's five levels of customer attitude (lowest to highest):

  1. Changes brands, especially for price: no loyalty
  2. Satisfied: no reason to change
  3. Satisfied and would incur costs by switching
  4. Values the brand and sees it as a friend
  5. Devoted to the brand

Brand equity is high when many customers are at levels 3, 4 and 5. It also depends on name recognition, perceived quality, strong mental and emotional associations, and assets such as patents, trademarks and channel relationships.

1. To brand or not

Branding costs money but gives:

  • legal protection (trademark)
  • a loyal, profitable customer base to face competition
  • the ability to segment the market
  • a corporate image that makes new launches easier

Distributors want brands because they're easier to handle and signal quality. Customers use brands to identify quality and shop efficiently.

2. Brand sponsor

Option Meaning
Manufacturer (national) brand The producer's own brand
Distributor (private, store, reseller) brand A retailer or wholesaler brand made under contract. More profitable (low production cost from excess capacity; low R&D, ad and distribution costs) and differentiates the store
Licensed brand Using another's name for a fee
Mixed Some under the maker's name, some under reseller labels

3. Brand name strategy

Strategy Meaning Example
Individual names Each product has its own name. The company's reputation isn't tied to it, so a failure doesn't hurt the firm Ceylon Cold Stores: Orange Crush, Necto, Lemonade, Ride
Blanket family name Everything under one name. Saves advertising; a good name helps new products Johnson & Johnson
Separate family names Different families for very different product groups A firm with separate food and cleaning families
Company name + individual name The company name legitimises; the individual name individualises Nestlé Maggi Noodles / Soups

Qualities of a good brand name:

Quality Example
Suggests product benefits "Samaposha"
Suggests product qualities (action, colour) "Perlwhite" laundry soap
Easy to pronounce, recognise, remember "Rin"
Distinctive "Kodak", "Fuji"
No poor meaning in other languages "Nova" means "doesn't go" in Spanish

Process: brainstorm a list, debate it, then test with target customers.

5. Brand repositioning

New competition or changing preferences may force a brand to reposition.

Example: 7 Up started as a mild lemon drink for older people. Research showed many soft-drink drinkers didn't want cola all the time, so 7 Up repositioned as "the Uncola": youthful, refreshing, the alternative to colas.

7Packaging & labelling

Some marketers call packaging the "fifth P"; others treat it as part of product strategy.

  • Packaging is designing and producing the container, with up to three levels: primary (immediate container), secondary (outer box), shipping packaging.
  • Packaging grew in importance because of self-service, consumer affluence, company and brand image, and innovation opportunities. It gives convenience and promotional value.

Packaging decisions:

  1. Define the packaging concept (what the package should be and do).
  2. Decide size, shape, materials, colour, text, brand, consistent with price and advertising.
  3. Test it: - Engineering: does it survive normal conditions? - Visual: legible, harmonious colours? - Dealer: attractive and easy to handle? - Consumer: a favourable response?
  4. Consider environmental and safety concerns.

Labels range from simple tags to elaborate graphics that are part of the package. Some carry only the brand; others carry lots of information, some of it required by law.
