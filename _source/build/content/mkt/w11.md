## Price: the revenue P

- **Pricing** is how a firm sets the amount it sells a product for. **Price** is the amount the consumer pays.
- Managers consider the **purchase or manufacturing cost, competition, market conditions, brand and quality**.

:::key Why price is special
**Price is the only one of the 4 Ps that generates income.** Product, place and promotion are **cost centres**. But good product, place and promotion **lower price elasticity**, so the firm can **raise prices** profitably.
:::

## Factors affecting pricing decisions

:::grid
**Internal factors**

- **Marketing and pricing objectives**: e.g. a target return on capital means revenue must exceed cost by enough
- **Marketing-mix strategy**: price must fit the other Ps; use research and the MIS
- **Costs**: price below cost means a loss. Cutting production cost allows a lower price
- **Organisational structure**: top management sets pricing policy; marketing managers and **cost accountants** (cost data) contribute; some firms involve workers
- **Product differentiation**: unique design, style or package means a higher price (e.g. **Emerald** and **Signature** shirts)
+++
**External factors**

- **Nature of the market and demand**: no demand means no sale; strong demand can be used
- **Competition**: many local and foreign products; without differentiation you can't charge more than rivals
- **Economy and government**: depressed economy means lower prices, boom means higher. Government **regulates** unfair practices (Nestlé's "free Kit Kat with Milo" offer was cancelled and penalised because the Kit Kat price was hidden in Milo's price)
- **Willingness to pay**: buyers' characteristics and preferences
- **Positioning and channels**: **longer distribution chains add more margin**; allow for discounts and concessions
:::

## The 6-step pricing process

A firm must set a price when it **launches a new product**, enters a **new channel**, or **bids** for contract work.

:::flow
1. Select the pricing objective
2. Determine demand
3. Estimate costs
4. Analyse competitors' costs, prices & offers
5. Select a pricing method
6. Select the final price
:::

### 1. Select the pricing objective
**The clearer the objective, the easier the price.**

| Objective | Pricing implication |
|---|---|
| **Survival** | **Cut prices** to raise volume against severe competition. Short-term only: costs aren't covered in the long run |
| **Current profit maximisation** | The price that gives the **maximum current profit** |
| **Market skimming** | **High** prices to skim the market |
| **Market-share maximisation** | **As low as possible** to become the **market leader** |
| **Product-quality leadership** | **Higher** prices to cover quality and **R&D**, and to signal better quality |

### 2. Determine demand
- **Price sensitivity:** a higher price usually means lower demand and a shift to **substitutes**.
- **Demand curve:** the statistical relationship between price and quantity demanded. Normally **inverse**.
- **Price elasticity of demand:**

:::formula
**Price elasticity = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price**
:::

- **Elastic** (|E| > 1): demand changes a lot with price.
- **Inelastic** (|E| < 1): demand barely changes.

Demand curves are measured through **surveys, price experiments and statistical analysis**.

### 3. Estimate costs
- **Fixed and variable costs:** price must cover both. Costs **fall as production rises** (bulk materials, experience), so estimate costs at **different output levels**.
- **Activity-based costing (ABC):** different retailers cost different amounts to serve.
- **Target costing:** start from the **price the product can sell at**, subtract the **desired margin**, and you get the **cost target** that design, engineering and production must hit.

### 4. Analyse competitors
**Benchmark** your price, quality and competitors' costs, using public data and statements. When a competitor changes its price, there are **three responses**:

1. **Maintain the status quo** (don't respond)
2. **Match** their price
3. **Undercut** them

### 5. Select a pricing method

| Basis | Method | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost-based** | **Cost-plus (mark-up)** | Price = average cost + mark-up %. **P = AVC + AVC × M**. The most common in manufacturing. **+** minimal information, simple, protects against cost changes. **−** **ignores competitors and customers** |
| | **Target-return** | Price that gives the **desired return on invested capital** |
| **Customer-based** | **Good-value pricing** | The **right mix of quality and service at a fair price** |
| | **Value-added (perceived-value) pricing** | **Add features and services** to differentiate, then **charge more** |
| **Competitor-based** | **Going-rate pricing** | Benchmark on competitors' prices (airlines on the same route; textbook publishers) |
| | **Sealed-bid pricing** | Bid on what competitors are expected to bid; the **lowest price wins** |

:::formula
**Target-return price = unit cost + (desired return × invested capital) ÷ expected unit sales**
:::

:::warn Note on the PDF
The PDF writes the target-return formula as "Desired Return × Invested Capital ÷ Expected Unit Sales" without the **unit cost** term. The standard formula (Kotler) adds the per-unit return **to the unit cost**, as shown above.
:::

### 6. Select the final price
Pricing methods narrow the range. The final choice also considers:

- the **impact of other marketing activities**
- **company pricing policies**
- **gain-and-risk-sharing pricing**
- the **impact on other parties** (distributors, competitors, government)

## New-product pricing: skimming vs penetration

| | **Price skimming** | **Market-penetration pricing** |
|---|---|---|
| **Idea** | Set a **high initial price** to skim maximum revenue from segments willing to pay, then **lower it step by step** | Set a **low initial price** to penetrate the market **quickly and deeply** and win **share** |
| **Result** | **Fewer but more profitable** sales | **Large volume and share**, lower profit per unit; volume cuts costs, allowing even lower prices |
| **Conditions** | **Quality and image support** a high price; **enough buyers** at that price; **competitors can't easily enter** and undercut | Market is **highly price-sensitive**; **economies of scale** are possible; the low price **keeps competitors out**; the firm must **keep** its low-price position |
| **Example** | **Apple's first iPhone**: high price, cut after about 6 months, cut again within a year | Low-priced phones or FMCG brands entering a mass market |

## Seven price-adjustment strategies

| Strategy | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Discount & allowance pricing** | Reduce prices to **reward responses**: **early payment, bulk purchase, off-season buying**. A **discount** is a direct reduction. **Allowances**: **trade-in** (cars), **promotional allowances** for dealers who advertise or support sales | 2% off for paying within 10 days |
| **2. Segmented pricing** | Two or more prices **not based on cost differences**: **customer-segment** (students, seniors), **product-form**, **location** (cinema seats), **time** | Museum student rates; cinema seat classes |
| **3. Psychological pricing** | Price **says something about the product**; many buyers **judge quality by price** | Rs 1,000 perfume containing Rs 300 of scent; Rs 999 instead of Rs 1,000 |
| **4. Promotional pricing** | **Temporary** prices below list (sometimes below cost) to boost **short-run sales** or clear stock. **Risks**: shoppers wait for sales, and the brand looks cheap | Seasonal sales |
| **5. Geographical pricing** | Prices for **different locations**, linked to buying power and freight | See the five methods below |
| **6. Dynamic and internet pricing** | Prices **change continually** (daily or hourly) with **demand, inventory, costs**; online **auctions** (eBay) | Airline seats, ride-hailing surge pricing |
| **7. International pricing** | Prices per country based on its **economy, competition, laws, distribution system**, or a uniform world price | Different phone prices across countries |

**Five geographical pricing methods:**

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **FOB-origin** | Goods placed **free on board**; the **buyer pays freight** from the factory, so prices differ by location |
| **Uniform-delivered** | **Same price + freight** for everyone, regardless of location |
| **Zone pricing** | Two or more zones; **same price within a zone**, higher for distant zones |
| **Basing-point** | Freight charged **from a chosen base city** to the customer |
| **Freight-absorption** | The seller **absorbs some or all freight** to win the business |

## Price changes & reactions

### Initiating price cuts
- **Why:** **excess capacity**, **falling demand** under strong price competition, a **weak economy**, or to **dominate through lower costs** and gain share and volume. *Example:* **Lenovo** mobiles' aggressive low prices in developing markets.
- **Risk:** **price wars**, and lower margins.

### Initiating price increases
- **Why:** **cost inflation**, or **demand exceeding supply** (e.g. the oil and gas industry).

:::example The power of a 1% price rise
If the profit margin is **3% of sales**, a **1% price increase** (with volume unchanged) raises profits by **1/3 ≈ 33%**.

*Check:* sales Rs 100 → profit Rs 3. A 1% increase gives sales Rs 101 at the same cost, so profit is Rs 4, which is +33%.
:::

**Avoid looking like a price gouger.** Consumers have long memories, and gouging can bring regulation.

- **Explain** the reasons for the increase.
- **Find alternatives:** shrink the product (smaller pack), use cheaper ingredients, remove features or services, or price previously bundled elements separately.

### Buyer and competitor reactions
**Price and image are closely linked:**

- A **Rolex price increase** may signal more exclusivity or better quality.
- A **Rolex price cut** may signal **lower quality** and a dulled luxury image.

So **price cuts can hurt how customers see the brand**.
