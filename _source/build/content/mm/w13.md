## Sound and digital representation

Audio communicates through **symbolic sounds**, **speech** and **music**. It can carry an explanation, signal a warning, attract attention or establish a mood. Choose sound that supports the message. A spoken Tamil instruction may help a learner follow a demonstration, while an unrelated loud soundtrack may hide the instruction.

Sound begins with vibrating matter. A **source** supplies vibration, a **medium** carries it, and a **detector** receives it. A loudspeaker moves air; pressure changes reach the ear. A microphone turns such variation into an electrical signal. The continuous waveform is analogue. A computer needs discrete numerical data.

:::key Sampling and quantization
**Sampling** measures the waveform at discrete time intervals. **Sampling rate** is the number of samples per second. **Quantization** maps each measured amplitude to an available discrete level. **Bit depth** determines how many levels are available.
:::

:::flow Redrawing of Figure 13.1
Microphone → analogue signal
ADC → sampling and quantization
DSP → processing, filtering or compression
Storage or transmission → encoded data
DSP → decoding and playback processing
DAC → analogue signal → speaker
:::

An **ADC** converts analogue input into digital samples. A **DAC** converts digital samples back into an analogue output. A **DSP** performs digital signal processing such as filtering, effects, compression or decompression. Capturing acquires incoming audio; recording additionally saves it. Live processing can capture sound without permanently recording it.

## Attributes: keep physical and digital quantities separate

| Attribute | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Waveform frequency | Cycles of physical oscillation per second, in Hz |
| Sampling rate | Numerical measurements per second, in samples/s or Hz |
| Period | Time for a periodic cycle; P = 1/f |
| Bit depth or sound resolution | Bits per sample; more bits allow finer amplitude steps |
| Channels | Independent streams: mono has one, stereo has two |
| Amplitude | Magnitude of a signal's variation; represented by sample values digitally |
| Pitch | Perceived highness or lowness, related mainly to frequency |
| Loudness | Perceived sound strength, related to intensity and perception |
| Bandwidth | Range of frequencies carried or retained |
| Dynamic range | Difference between high and low levels |

The PDF lists infrasonic sound as **0–20 Hz**, typical human hearing as **20 Hz–20 kHz**, ultrasound as **20 kHz–1 GHz**, and hypersound as **1 GHz–10 THz**. Hearing limits vary; these are the lesson's classification ranges, not a promise that every listener hears the whole range.

**Intensity** is power per area, measured in **W/m²**. The lesson uses **10^-12 W/m²** as the reference threshold of hearing and **1 W/m²** as a threshold-of-feeling example. Its relative intensity formula is **dB = 10 log10(I₂/I₁)**. If an orchestra ranges from **30 dB** to **130 dB**, its example dynamic range is **130 − 30 = 100 dB**.

:::warn Source corrections and limits
The PDF's first definition under “Frequency” actually describes **sampling rate**, not the sound waveform's frequency. A higher sampling rate alone does not guarantee clearer audio; capture quality, source bandwidth, noise and processing also matter. Stereo provides two channels and can support spatial cues; mono is often appropriate for a single clear voice. The lesson's suggested normalisation to **80%–90%** is a teaching example, not a universal delivery loudness standard.
:::

## Sampling rate and Nyquist: solved examples

:::formula
For the lesson's reconstruction condition: sampling rate fₛ > 2 × highest retained frequency fₘₐₓ
Nyquist frequency = fₛ ÷ 2
Sample count per channel = fₛ × time
Sampling interval = 1 ÷ fₛ seconds
:::

The source uses **more than twice** the highest frequency. At the ideal theoretical boundary, additional assumptions matter; in practice leave a transition band for an anti-alias filter. Before reducing sampling rate, remove frequency content that would exceed the new limit. Otherwise it can alias into misleading lower frequencies.

:::example Three Nyquist examples
**A:** retain sound up to **8 kHz**. Formula → fₛ > 2fₘₐₓ. Substitution → **fₛ > 2 × 8 = 16 kHz**. A chosen **22.05 kHz** rate satisfies this condition.

**B:** at **44.1 kHz**, Nyquist frequency = **44.1 ÷ 2 = 22.05 kHz**. Under the lesson's strict wording, retain frequencies below that boundary.

**C:** retain frequencies up to **20 kHz**. Requirement → **fₛ > 2 × 20 = 40 kHz**. A chosen **48 kHz** rate satisfies it; **32 kHz** does not.
:::

:::example Three sample-count examples
**A:** **8,000 samples/s** for **3 s**. N = fₛt = **8,000 × 3 = 24,000 samples per channel**.

**B:** **44,100 samples/s** for **60 s**. N = **44,100 × 60 = 2,646,000 samples per channel**.

**C:** **48,000 samples/s** for **2 s**. N = **48,000 × 2 = 96,000 samples per channel**. Interval = **1/48,000 s ≈ 20.833 microseconds**.
:::

The source's sampling-rate table is historical. Its labels include **8,000 Hz** telephony/UNIX, **11,000 Hz** quarter-CD-era Macintosh, **16,000 Hz** G.722, **18,900 Hz** CD-ROM XA, **22,000 Hz** half-CD-era Macintosh, **32,000 Hz** HDTV/long-play DAT, **37,800 Hz** CD XA, **44,056 Hz** professional audio, **44,100 Hz** CD, and **48,000 Hz** DAT. The quarter and half CD labels are rounded source values, rather than exact fractions of the CD rate. Use the explicitly stated rate in a calculation.

## Bit depth, channels and raw capacity

:::formula
Quantization levels = 2^b, where b is bits/sample
Audio bytes = C × S × T × B
C = channels; S = sampling rate in Hz; T = seconds; B = bits/sample ÷ 8
PCM bit rate = C × S × bits/sample
Decimal MB = bytes ÷ 1,000,000
:::

This is an **uncompressed PCM payload** estimate. It excludes headers and metadata. A compressed format may use a specified encoded bit rate instead. Keep bits and bytes separate. Doubling duration, channels, bit depth or sampling rate doubles this raw capacity when all other quantities remain fixed.

:::example Three bit-depth examples
**A:** **8-bit** audio has **2^8 = 256 levels** and **8/8 = 1 byte/sample**.

**B:** **16-bit** audio has **2^16 = 65,536 levels** and **16/8 = 2 bytes/sample**.

**C:** **24-bit** audio has **2^24 = 16,777,216 levels** and **24/8 = 3 bytes/sample**. This extra worked depth extends the PDF's examples, which mention **8-bit, 16-bit and 32-bit**.
:::

:::example Three size examples, including Activity 13.1
**A, PDF example:** **1 minute**, **44.1 kHz**, **16-bit**, stereo.
Formula → C × S × T × B. Substitution → **2 × 44,100 × 60 × 2**.
Working → **10,584,000 bytes**. Answer → **10.584 MB ≈ 10.6 MB**, using decimal MB as in the PDF.

**B, Activity 13.1:** calculate CD-rate, **2-minute**, **16-bit mono** capacity.
Use **S = 44,100 Hz**, **T = 2×60 = 120 s**, **C = 1**, **B = 16/8 = 2**.
Working → **1 × 44,100 × 120 × 2 = 10,584,000 bytes = 10.584 MB**. “CD quality” supplies the sampling rate here; the question explicitly requests mono.

**C:** **10 s**, **48,000 Hz**, **24-bit**, stereo.
Working → **2 × 48,000 × 10 × 3 = 2,880,000 bytes = 2.88 MB**.
:::

:::example Three channel and bit-rate examples
**A:** **8,000 Hz**, **8-bit mono**. Rate = **1 × 8,000 × 8 = 64,000 bit/s = 64 kb/s**.

**B:** **44,100 Hz**, **16-bit stereo**. Rate = **2 × 44,100 × 16 = 1,411,200 bit/s = 1.4112 Mb/s**.

**C:** **48,000 Hz**, **16-bit** audio for **5 s**. Mono size = **1 × 48,000 × 5 × 2 = 480,000 bytes**. Stereo size = **2 × 48,000 × 5 × 2 = 960,000 bytes**. Channel ratio = **960,000/480,000 = 2**.
:::

## Capturing and multitrack production: Activity 13.2

Microphone choice and placement affect the captured signal. The PDF mentions omnidirectional and clip-on microphones. Capture may be live, single-track or multitrack. A production can involve vocalists, instrumentalists, editors and effects specialists. Monitor the input and leave usable headroom; a distorted recording is harder to repair than a clean recording.

**Question:** research and describe multitrack music production.

**Model answer:** plan the arrangement, tempo, instruments and voices. Prepare the recording environment and connect microphones or instruments to suitable inputs. Create a session and separate labelled tracks. Set input levels and monitor a test recording. Record a guide or rhythmic reference, then record performances on their own tracks while performers hear the required reference. Review takes and select or assemble the desired performances. Align and trim clips without damaging musical timing. Adjust levels and panning, apply appropriate equalization and effects, and balance the mix. Listen on more than one playback system, revise, and export a master plus a delivery version. Save the session and sources for revision. The [Adobe Audition learning collection](https://helpx.adobe.com/audition/tutorials.html) provides the course's practical starting point; its linked tutorials cover recording, editing and multitrack work.

## Filtering, encoding and Activity 13.3

A **high-pass filter** retains higher frequencies and attenuates low-frequency material such as rumble. A **low-pass filter** retains lower frequencies and attenuates higher ones. **Amplitude response** describes a filter's gain at each frequency. Filters can be implemented through time-domain or frequency-domain processing.

:::warn Source limit
The PDF says filters only attenuate and cannot boost. That describes its simple cleanup examples, not every audio filter or equalizer. Equalization can cut or boost selected ranges. “Higher sampling rate → higher quality” is also a simplification; it does increase raw capacity at fixed settings.
:::

**Encoding** represents audio for storage or transmission. Compression reduces data. **Lossless** encoding reconstructs the original digital data. **Lossy** encoding discards some information. Choose according to required fidelity, storage, bandwidth, platform and editing needs.

**Activity 13.3 question:** list lossy and lossless audio formats.

| Category | Model answer | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Lossy | MP3, AAC, Vorbis, Opus | Smaller delivery copies discard some information |
| Lossless compression | FLAC, ALAC | Original numerical samples can be reconstructed |
| Common uncompressed audio | PCM in WAV or AIFF | Uncompressed is not the same as lossless compression |

A container can support different encodings. Verify the actual codec rather than judging only by its filename extension.

## Editing, effects, mixing and Activity 13.4

| Operation from the PDF | What it does |
|---|---|
| Trimming | Removes unwanted silence or material at boundaries |
| Splicing and assembly | Cuts, joins and arranges recordings |
| Volume adjustment | Balances level; excessive gain can clip |
| Format conversion | Saves an appropriate new representation |
| Resampling/downsampling | Changes sampling rate; filter before reducing bandwidth |
| Fade-in/fade-out | Gradually increases/decreases level |
| Equalization | Adjusts selected frequency ranges |
| Time stretching | Changes duration while aiming to preserve pitch |
| Digital signal processing | Creates effects such as pitch changes, robot voice, echo or reverb |
| Reversing | Plays samples in the opposite order |

The lesson names Audition, Sound Forge, GoldWave and Audacity as editing examples. These are course examples rather than current licensing advice. When combining sound with video, align speech with the picture, keep dialogue understandable, reduce competing music, check peaks and transitions, and inspect the exported result. Preserve clean original files.

**Activity 13.4 question:** add echo to an existing audio file with Audition or similar software.

**Model procedure:** duplicate the source or use a non-destructive effects session. Import a short spoken phrase. Select an echo/delay effect and preview it. Choose an audible delay, moderate feedback and a wet/dry balance that preserves intelligibility. Compare bypassed and processed sound at similar level. Lower output if peaks clip. Leave room after the phrase so the echo tail is not cut off, then export a separate processed copy and listen again. Keep the original and project settings. This procedure is the model answer; no fabricated claim is made that a source recording was supplied or edited.

The [Adobe delay and echo reference](https://helpx.adobe.com/audition/using/delay-echo-effects.html) explains that delay time separates repeats, wet/dry controls balance processed and original sound, and feedback affects repeated decay. It also advises allowing sufficient tail duration. Echo gives distinct repeats; reverb suggests many blended reflections.

:::exam English answer frame
“Digitization samples an analogue waveform at regular time intervals and quantizes each sample's amplitude. Sampling rate controls time sampling, while bit depth controls available amplitude levels. For uncompressed audio, size equals channels × sampling rate × seconds × bytes per sample. The rate must exceed twice the highest retained frequency under the lesson's reconstruction condition.”
:::
