1Why study consumer behaviour?

  • Organisations exist to satisfy consumers' needs and wants, and they can only do that by understanding the people who buy, better than competitors do.
  • We can't open a head to read the thinking process, so marketers study behaviour instead: behaviour is the outward sign of mental intentions.
  • Understanding behaviour makes it easy to match products to needs.

2Consumer markets

  • A consumer market is the system through which individuals and households buy goods and services for personal consumption, not resale. Buying groceries or paying for a car wash both count.
  • The more people actively buy, the more active the consumer market.

Consumers differ in tastes and habits along four sets of characteristics (the same as the segmentation bases):

Characteristic Includes Example
Demographic Gender, age, ethnicity, income, occupation, education, household size, religion, generation, nationality, social class Age bands 18–24, 25–34, 35–54, 55–65, 65+; a phone aimed at 18–24-year-olds on mid incomes
Psychographic Interests, activities, opinions, values, attitudes Prenatal magazines for expectant mothers; martial-arts gear; insights from focus groups
Behaviouristic Usage rate, brand loyalty, user status, benefits sought Heavy, medium and light users; brand loyalists
Geographic Market size, region, population density, climate Beachwear sells more in warm climates; regional food tastes

3The black box model (stimulus → response)

 STIMULI                      BUYER'S BLACK BOX                     RESPONSES
 Marketing: Product,     →   Buyer characteristics:          →   Product choice
   Price, Place,              Cultural · Social                   Brand choice
   Promotion                  Personal · Psychological            Dealer choice
 Other: Economic,             Buyer decision process:             Purchase timing
   Technological,             Problem recognition → Search →      Purchase amount
   Political, Cultural        Evaluation → Decision → Post-purchase
  • Stimuli: the 4 Ps plus other environmental stimuli (economic, technological, political, cultural).
  • Desired responses: buying or not buying a product, buying at a particular place, buying from a particular dealer.
  • The challenge: people don't respond uniformly to the same stimuli at different times, because of the buyer's black box: their characteristics and decision process. Marketers try to understand what goes on inside it.

4Factors influencing consumer behaviour

🧪 Try it — Explore the four groups of influences

1. Cultural factors

  • Culture is the most fundamental determinant of buyer behaviour: the sum of learned beliefs, values and customs that direct the behaviour of a society's members.
  • Subculture is a more homogeneous group within a culture, with its own values, customs and traditions.
  • Social class is a relatively homogeneous, enduring, hierarchically ordered division whose members share values, interests and behaviour.

2. Social factors

Reference groups influence attitudes and behaviour, directly or indirectly:

Reference groups
├── Membership groups
│     ├── Primary   (family, friends, neighbours: frequent, informal)
│     └── Secondary (clubs, professional bodies: formal, less frequent)
└── Non-membership groups
      ├── Aspirational groups   (you want to belong)
      └── Dissociative groups   (you want to avoid)

They influence buyers in three ways:

  1. Expose the person to new behaviours and lifestyles.
  2. Influence attitudes and self-concept.
  3. Create pressure to conform, which affects product and brand choices.
  • Family is the most important consumer buying organisation and the nucleus of buying units. It socialises people and instils buying habits.
  • Roles and statuses: the roles people play give them status, which influences purchases.

3. Personal factors

  • Age and life-cycle stage
  • Occupation and economic circumstances: spendable income, savings, debts, borrowing power, attitude to spending vs saving
  • Lifestyle: the pattern of living in activities, interests and opinions (Kotler, 2000)
  • Personality and self-concept: distinguishing psychological characteristics leading to relatively consistent, enduring responses (Kotler, 2000). Consumers express their personality through products.

4. Psychological factors

  • Motivation: a need drives goal-directed action. Consumers are need-driven. Theories: Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Herzberg's two-factor theory, McClelland's need theory.
  • Learning: changes in behaviour arising from experience.
  • Beliefs and attitudes:
  • A belief is a descriptive thought.
  • An attitude is an enduring favourable or unfavourable evaluation, feeling and action tendency towards an object or idea.
  • Perception: the internal representation of the external world. "Consumer perception should be the marketer's reality."
Buying roles

Initiator (suggests the purchase) → Influencer → Decider → Buyer → User → Discarder.

Example: a child asks for a laptop (initiator); a cousin recommends a brand (influencer); a parent decides (decider) and pays (buyer); the child uses it (user) and eventually disposes of it (discarder).

5The buyer decision process — 5 stages

  1. Need / problem recognition
  1. Information search
  1. Evaluation of alternatives
  1. Purchase decision
  1. Post-purchase behaviour
Stage What happens Marketer's job
1. Need recognition The buyer feels a gap between their actual and desired state ("I'm hungry, I need food"). Triggered by internal stimuli (hunger, thirst) or external stimuli (ads, word of mouth) Trigger and remind of the need
2. Information search Personal sources (family, friends, neighbours), commercial (ads, salespeople, dealers, packaging, displays), public (mass media, consumer-rating organisations), experiential (handling, examining, using: the most decisive and trusted) Be present in the sources customers trust
3. Evaluation of alternatives Brands in the choice set are compared on attributes, importance, brand beliefs and expected satisfaction Show how your brand wins on the attributes that matter
4. Purchase decision Buy the best brand. But the decision is affected by others' attitudes and unexpected situational factors Remove barriers; use point-of-sale persuasion
5. Post-purchase behaviour Satisfied or dissatisfied. Cognitive dissonance: "Did I make the right decision?" Reassure buyers: follow-ups, warranties, good after-sales service

6Four types of buying behaviour

The type depends on involvement (how important or expensive the purchase is) and differences between brands:

High involvement Low involvement
Significant differences between brands Complex buying behaviour Variety-seeking behaviour
Few differences between brands Dissonance-reducing behaviour Habitual buying behaviour
Type Description Example Marketer's response
Complex Highly involved + big brand differences. The buyer gathers detailed information Motorcycle, laptop, diamond necklace Provide detailed information on attributes; educate
Variety-seeking Low involvement + significant differences. Switches brands for variety, not dissatisfaction Trying a different washing detergent or biscuit each time Market leader: dominate shelf space and remind. Challengers: discounts, free samples, heavy advertising
Dissonance-reducing High involvement + few differences. Buys fairly quickly, then may feel dissonance Floor tiles, carpets After-sale reassurance about the choice
Habitual Low involvement + few differences. Buys quickly out of habit Toothpaste, salt, sugar Price promotions, repetition, availability

7Contemporary trends — Sri Lanka & global

Sri Lanka (Nielsen consumer insights, 2015–2017)

  1. Consumer confidence fell: from 87 (Oct 2015) to 46 (Jul 2016), recovering to 54 (Dec 2016), then flat. 80% expected job prospects not to improve, 90% expected unsatisfactory personal finances, and 92% expected purchasing power to fall.
  2. Rising food, utility and education costs: people cut usage of personal care (e.g. shampoo), and penetration fell in some hygiene categories. FMCG firms missed their budgets.
  3. Top three issues: political stability, utility bills, children's education costs. Drought hit 14 districts, and imported basics (rice, dhal, sprats, sugar) rose with currency swings.
  4. Moving away from supermarkets: supermarket shoppers fell from 23% (2015) to 19% (2016), while grocery shopping rose from 46% to 53%. Consumers became more rational.
  5. Local companies growing: 67% of incremental sales came from local firms vs 55% for multinationals, helped by quality parity.
  6. Internet use rising to 32% penetration, with companies spending proportionately more on digital media.
